

### **Chapter 6: Vance Global Holdings**

**February 20, 2026**

The world Mohamed Vance intended to build could not rest on a foundation of sand. It required bedrock. For two nights, after his shifts at Keen and before the city of Louisville had fully woken, he sat not in front of his code editor but in the labyrinthine digital corridors of corporate law. He consumed articles on liability shields, tax implications, and the subtle art of anonymity. He learned the language of registered agents, operating agreements, and piercing the corporate veil. His apartment, usually filled with the low hum of his server rack and the scent of stale coffee, took on the sterile, quiet atmosphere of a law library.

He was an engineer. He saw the world as a series of interconnected systems, and the legal and financial worlds were no different. They were simply older, more complex systems, written in arcane language and executed by human beings instead of silicon. But they had rules. They had exploits. And they had architectures.

His current setup was a vulnerability. VanceFlow and VancePack were tied to a simple Stripe account under his own name. His freelance contracts were paid to Mohamed Vance. He was exposed, a single point of failure. If anyone—a competitor, a curious journalist, a government agency—ever decided to look closely at the unusual success of his small software projects, they would find him. A machinist in Kentucky with a high school diploma and an inexplicable knack for creating hyper-efficient code. That was a loose thread, and he was beginning to understand that in the game he was preparing to play, a single loose thread could unravel everything.

He needed layers. He needed abstraction. He needed a structure that would allow capital and information to flow where he directed, while his own identity remained shielded, a ghost behind the curtain.

The answer, as it was for so many, was Delaware. The state was a haven of corporate ambiguity, its laws designed to protect owners and directors. He sketched it out on his whiteboard, the same one that held the sprawling architecture for VanceFlow. This time, the diagram wasn't of data pipelines and user interfaces, but of legal entities.

At the top, in a clean, bold box, he wrote: **VANCE GLOBAL HOLDINGS, LLC**.

A holding company. It wouldn't produce anything. It wouldn't sell anything. Its sole purpose was to own other things. It was a shield, a buffer, a name on paper that would exist in a filing cabinet in Wilmington. He would be the sole member, but his name would not be on the public documents. For that, he needed a registered agent.

He found a reputable firm online, one that specialized in exactly this kind of service. For a few hundred dollars a year, they would provide a legal address in Delaware, accept official mail on his behalf, and file the necessary paperwork with the Secretary of State, listing their own name as the organizer. To the outside world, Vance Global Holdings would be an entity managed by a generic corporate services firm.

Beneath the holding company, he drew a second box, connected by a solid line. **VANCETECH, LLC**.

This would be a Kentucky-based LLC, a subsidiary of the Delaware parent. VanceTech would be the operational arm. It would own the intellectual property for VanceFlow and VancePack. It would be the entity that signed contracts, opened a proper business bank account, and eventually, hired employees. It would be public-facing, but anyone who tried to trace its ownership would hit the dead end of Vance Global Holdings in Delaware. It was a simple two-layer structure, but for now, it was a fortress compared to his current exposure.

The cost was a sharp intake of breath. The Delaware formation fees, the registered agent service for the first year, and the Kentucky filing fees would come to just over eight hundred dollars. It was a fifth of his entire liquid net worth. It was money he’d painstakingly accumulated through a combination of his machinist’s salary, a few small but successful crypto trades, and the first trickles of revenue from his software. Eight hundred dollars was groceries for three months. It was his entire emergency fund.

He stared at the numbers in his bank account, then at the diagram on the whiteboard. One represented the present, a precarious and limited reality. The other represented the future, a structure designed for immense scale. The System had given him access to a power he was only beginning to comprehend, but it operated in a currency—SP—that was useless for building the mundane, essential scaffolding of the real world. He had to use his own sweat and blood, his fiat dollars, to lay the foundation.

With the grim determination of a soldier digging a foxhole under fire, he navigated to the legal services website. He filled out the forms, his fingers moving with the same precision they used on a keyboard or a CNC controller. Name of entity: Vance Global Holdings, LLC. Organizer: The registered agent’s firm. He double-checked every field, then triple-checked. He uploaded a scan of his driver’s license to their secure portal for identity verification—a necessary evil. Then he entered his debit card number.

The click of the ‘Submit’ button was deafening in the silent apartment. $804.37 vanished from his account. A wave of nausea, cold and sharp, washed over him. It was the most money he had ever spent on something so intangible. It wasn't a car, or a tool, or even a piece of hardware. It was a concept, a legal fiction.

He leaned back in his chair, the worn leather groaning in protest. His fiat balance now stood at a little over $3,300. Precariously low. But the foundation was being poured. He had purchased his first piece of armor.

---

Two days later, a crisp email arrived with the subject line: “Certificate of Formation – Vance Global Holdings, LLC.” He opened the attached PDF. There it was, stamped and official. His ghost was born.

The birth of his company coincided with another, more pressing realization. He was drowning. Not in debt or despair, but in work. The user base for his applications was now approaching ten thousand people. That meant ten thousand potential sources of bug reports, feature requests, and support tickets. His inbox was a relentless tide of human need. He was spending three hours a day just answering emails, troubleshooting minor issues, and placating users who couldn’t figure out a simple workflow. That was three hours he wasn't spending coding, studying, or planning.

The System rewarded growth, but growth created friction. His time, his focus, was his most valuable resource, more precious than SP or dollars, and it was being eroded by a thousand tiny cuts.

He thought of Danielle Jones.

Her invoice for the single consulting session sat on his desk, paid. Her feedback had been the catalyst for the v0.4 redesign he was currently working on. She had seen the flaws in his UX with a clarity that was both humbling and infuriating. She understood the user’s mind in a way he, the engineer, never could. She was a translator, bridging the gap between his logic and their experience.

Hiring her was the obvious, logical next step. It was also terrifying. It meant letting someone into his world. Not just as a one-off consultant, but as a consistent presence. It meant an ongoing expense, a commitment. It meant trusting someone.

He ran the numbers. To be competitive, to make it worth her while to dedicate significant time to him, he’d have to offer a respectable monthly retainer. He researched contractor rates for user research and customer support specialists in the tech industry. The figures were daunting. He couldn't afford a full-time employee, not even close. But a part-time contractor… maybe.

He settled on a number: $3,500 a month.

It was an insane figure. It was more than he made in a month at Keen after taxes. It would consume nearly every dollar of revenue his software was generating, forcing him to rely entirely on his machinist’s pay and careful trading to cover his own living expenses. It was a gamble of epic proportions, betting his entire operational cash flow on one person.

But what was the alternative? Stagnation. Burning out trying to be an engineer, a CEO, and a customer support department all at once. He was building an empire, not a lifestyle business. Empires required delegation. They required specialists.

He picked up his phone and found her number. He hesitated, his thumb hovering over the call button. He was about to offer a woman he’d met once a job that constituted the single largest expense of his nascent enterprise. A job that would require her to work from his small, cluttered apartment several days a week. He still wouldn’t be able to tell her about the System, about the true source of his insights, about the real scale of his ambition. He would have to trust her to be brilliant within the small, carefully curated box of information he provided.

He pressed the button.

“Danielle Jones.” Her voice was as crisp and professional as he remembered.

“Danielle, it’s Mohamed Vance.”

“Mohamed! Hi. I was wondering if I’d hear from you. Did the feedback help with the new version?”

“It did,” he said, the words feeling inadequate. “It did more than help. It reshaped it. Which is actually why I’m calling. I have a proposal for you. Are you free to talk for a few minutes?”

There was a brief pause. “I am. What’s on your mind?”

He took a breath, marshaling his thoughts into the logical, persuasive argument he’d rehearsed in his head. “My user base is growing faster than I can manage. I’m spending more time on support and user outreach than on development. It’s not sustainable. I was impressed with your work, with the way you think. I’d like to hire you on a contract basis.”

“Okay,” she said slowly, her tone intrigued. “What kind of scope are you thinking of?”

“I’d want you to take over all user-facing communication. Customer support, feedback collection, social media, the whole package. I also want you to conduct formal user research—interviews, surveys—to build a roadmap for future features. Essentially, I want you to become the voice of the user, and I’ll be the hands that build what you learn.”

Another pause, longer this time. He could hear the faint sound of typing in the background. She was likely looking him up, looking at the apps, the user counts, trying to gauge the size of the operation.

“That’s a significant role, Mohamed. For a project of this size, that sounds like a near full-time commitment.”

“I can’t offer a full-time salary,” he said, cutting straight to the point. “Not yet. But I can offer a monthly retainer of thirty-five hundred dollars. For, say, three days a week of your time. We can define the exact hours, but I need a consistent commitment.”

The silence on the other end of the line was absolute. He had either insulted her or stunned her. Given the rates for top-tier consultants, he feared it was the former. He was about to start backpedaling when she finally spoke, her voice carefully neutral.

“Thirty-five hundred a month. As a 1099 contractor.”

“That’s the offer,” he confirmed, his own voice tight.

“And where would I be working?”

This was the tricky part. “Ideally, here. At my apartment. At least for the first few weeks. The entire development environment is on a closed local network. It’s… secure. Once we have better cloud infrastructure and security protocols in place, we can discuss remote options. I know it’s not ideal.”

He was offering a substantial amount of money for a small, unknown project, but with the bizarre string attached that she had to work out of a stranger’s apartment in the Highlands. He expected her to laugh, to politely decline.

Instead, she said, “Send me a contract. I’ll have my lawyer look it over. If it’s standard, I can start on Monday.”

He was so taken aback he almost forgot to speak. “Monday? This coming Monday?”

“Is there a problem with that?”

“No. No problem at all. I’ll… I’ll draft something and send it over this afternoon.”

“Good. I’ll talk to you then, Mohamed.”

She hung up.

Mohamed lowered the phone, staring at the black screen. He had done it. He had just hired his first team member. He had also just committed to a monthly expense that could bankrupt him in sixty days if his revenue didn't continue to grow. He felt a dizzying mix of exhilaration and pure, unadulterated terror. The scaffolding was getting higher, and the fall was getting longer.

**February 27, 2026**

Danielle’s first day was an exercise in controlled awkwardness. She arrived at ten o’clock sharp, dressed in smart casual attire that made Mohamed acutely aware of his own worn jeans and faded t-shirt. She carried a sleek laptop bag and a reusable water bottle, radiating a calm professionalism that seemed to physically push back against the organized chaos of his living space.

“It’s… efficient,” she said, her eyes scanning the room. It was the most generous possible description for a one-bedroom apartment dominated by a massive desk, two monitors, a server rack humming in the corner, and a whiteboard covered in cryptic diagrams. His couch was a temporary holding area for notebooks and discarded coffee mugs. His kitchen table was his workbench for tinkering with electronics.

“It’s a workshop,” he replied, feeling a strange need to defend it.

“I get it,” she said, and he had the sense that she actually did. “Where do you want me?”

He had cleared a small section of his desk, a demilitarized zone between his primary monitor and his soldering station. It was a woefully inadequate space, but it was all he had. She set down her laptop without complaint, her movements economical and precise. She plugged it in, connected to his guest Wi-Fi, and looked at him. “Okay. Let’s start with a systems overview. Show me everything. Your support ticket system, your user database, your feedback channels, your code repository.”

The next hour was a blur. He walked her through his patchwork of solutions. Support requests came directly to a dedicated Gmail address, which he managed with a complex system of labels and filters. User feedback was scattered across that inbox, comments on a handful of tech forums, and direct messages on Twitter. His “user database” was little more than the raw data from his Stripe and PayPal accounts. He didn’t even bother showing her his code; she wasn’t a developer and it wasn’t relevant to her role. Yet.

She listened intently, her expression unreadable. She asked sharp, insightful questions that exposed the weaknesses in his ad-hoc processes. Why wasn't he using a proper ticketing system? How was he tracking recurring issues? Was he correlating feedback with user demographics? He found himself constantly saying, “I haven’t had time to set that up yet.”

By the end of it, he felt like a student whose sloppy homework was being reviewed by a master.

“Okay,” she said, leaning back in the chair he’d provided. It was his only other comfortable chair, a reading chair from the corner. His own was a high-backed ergonomic model. The disparity felt symbolic. “First things first. We need to centralize. Your current system is bleeding data and wasting your time.”

She didn’t wait for his permission. She simply started working. With a few clicks, she signed them up for a free tier of a professional help desk software. She methodically created rules to forward all his support emails into a new, unified ticketing queue. She then turned to his scattered notes on user feedback.

“We need a project management tool,” she declared. “Something visual. Trello, Asana, something like that. So we can turn feedback into actionable items and track them through the development cycle.”

Before he could even process the suggestion, she had a Trello board set up. She created columns: ‘Feedback Inbox,’ ‘Under Review,’ ‘Approved for Roadmap,’ ‘In Development,’ ‘Testing,’ ‘Launched.’ It was a simple, elegant visualization of the chaotic process that had, until then, existed only inside his head.

He watched, mesmerized. She was an architect of systems, just like him. But where he built with code and logic, she built with process and people. She was creating order from his chaos, and she was doing it without asking, because the need for it was self-evident. It was the exact same way he approached a poorly written piece of code—he didn’t ask for permission to refactor it, he just did it because it was the right way.

For the rest of the day, they worked in a strange, silent tandem. He coded, refining the UI for VanceFlow v0.4 based on her initial report. She worked on her own laptop, the rapid, quiet tapping of her keys a new rhythm in the apartment’s soundscape. She was clearing the backlog of support emails, migrating them into the new system, tagging and categorizing each one. She was building the infrastructure of customer relations while he built the infrastructure of the product itself.

He found he didn’t mind her presence. It was focused, unobtrusive. There was no small talk, no wasted motion. When she had a question, she asked it clearly and concisely. When she had a solution, she implemented it. It was like working with another version of himself, but one with a completely different, and complementary, skillset.

He left for his shift at Keen at 9 PM. When he returned at seven the next morning, exhausted and smelling of machine coolant, he found a sticky note on his monitor.

It read: *‘Coffee’s made. I set up a shared Slack workspace to keep our comms organized. Invite is in your email. See you tomorrow at 10.’*

He looked toward the kitchen. The coffee pot was full. A clean mug sat next to it. His apartment felt different. It was still his space, but it was no longer just his. An alien, organizing intelligence had been introduced into the ecosystem. And he found, to his profound surprise, that he was deeply, fundamentally okay with that.

---

The next two weeks fell into a rhythm. Danielle would come in Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday. She would work with quiet, relentless efficiency, transforming his customer-facing operations. She established a professional but friendly tone for all communications. She created a comprehensive FAQ that resolved nearly thirty percent of incoming support tickets without any manual intervention. She began conducting user interviews via video call, her calm, probing questions teasing out invaluable insights that he knew he would never have gotten on his own.

He watched her work, and he learned. She was teaching him, without ever explicitly saying so, how to run a company, not just how to write code.

One afternoon, he noticed her staring at the whiteboard, at his sprawling diagram of the Vance Global Holdings structure. He’d forgotten to erase it.

“Vance Global Holdings,” she said, reading the name aloud. “That’s ambitious.”

He felt a jolt of adrenaline, the reflexive secrecy tensing the muscles in his back. “It’s just a legal shield,” he said, his tone more defensive than he intended. “Standard practice.”

“Is it?” she asked, turning to look at him. Her gaze was unnervingly direct. “Most guys building an app in their apartment start with a simple sole proprietorship or a local LLC. A multi-state, two-layer holding structure… that’s for someone who is planning for a scale that is anything but standard.”

He didn’t reply. He just met her gaze. He didn’t confirm or deny. He let the silence hang in the air.

She held his eyes for a moment longer, then gave a small, almost imperceptible nod. She turned back to her laptop. The subject was dropped. But a message had been passed between them. She knew he was playing a bigger game. And she had signaled that she was okay with not knowing the rules, for now. The trust between them deepened, a silent pact made in the space between words.

He, in turn, began to trust her more. He gave her access to his user analytics, showing her the real-time data on adoption and engagement. She absorbed it instantly, her insights sharpening her research. They were becoming a closed loop, a two-person system. She would gather data from the world, refine it into intelligence, and feed it to him. He would convert that intelligence into code, into product, and release it back into the world. The cycle would repeat, faster and more efficiently each time.

They were two architects, he realized, who had discovered they were building the same cathedral from opposite sides. He was laying the stone and carving the intricate logic of its structure. She was designing the acoustics, ensuring the light would hit the altar just right, making it a place a person would actually want to be in. And neither of them could finish it without the other.

---

At Keen American Built, the world was simpler. It was a world of steel and tungsten carbide, of tolerances measured in microns and the high-pitched scream of a cutting tool shaping metal. He had switched to the 10 PM to 6 AM shift, the graveyard shift. It was a brutal schedule for most, but for him, it was perfect. It left his days free, his most productive hours protected from the demands of a normal job.

The cultivation, as he had begun to think of it, was changing him in ways that were becoming impossible to ignore. The most profound change was his sleep. He no longer needed eight hours. He would come home from his shift, sleep for five hours, and wake up feeling more rested and clear-headed than he ever had in his life. It was as if the efficiency of his mind was extending to his own biology, optimizing the restorative processes of his body. Those extra three hours a day were a gift, a secret advantage. It was twenty-one extra hours of work, of thought, of planning, every single week.

His mind was sharper, too. At work, he could visualize the G-code before he even ran the simulation. He could hear a subtle change in the pitch of the CNC mill and know, instinctively, that a tool was beginning to wear, long before the machine’s sensors would flag it. His hands were steadier. His focus, absolute.

One morning, just as his shift was ending, his supervisor, Derek Holloway, called him over. Derek was a lifer, a man whose hands were permanently stained with the ghost of machine oil and whose face was a roadmap of factory stress.

“Vance,” he said, holding up a newly milled component, a complex gear for a new robotics contract. “This is yours, right?”

“Yes, sir,” Mohamed said, his stomach tightening. He ran a quick mental check of the job. The specs were tight, some of the tolerances were down to a tenth of a thousandth of an inch.

Derek ran a calloused thumb over the polished surface. “I just had QA check this batch. Your pieces… they’re consistently twelve percent more precise than the line average. Twelve percent. That’s not a statistical anomaly, that’s a different class of work.”

Mohamed stayed silent. He didn’t know what to say. *I have a magic space-god system that is slowly rewriting my neurology for peak performance.*

Derek looked at him, his expression one of genuine curiosity. “You’re not on anything, are you, kid?”

“No, sir,” Mohamed said, his voice steady. “Just getting a lot of sleep.”

Derek grunted, a sound that could have meant anything. “Whatever it is, keep doing it. The front office is breathing down my neck about the rejection rate on this contract. Your work is basically saving my ass. I’m putting you in for a raise. Two-fifty an hour. It’s not what you’re worth, but it’s what I can get.”

“Thank you, Derek,” Mohamed said, genuinely surprised. An extra $2.50 an hour was another five thousand dollars a year. It was a significant buffer. It was another layer of security.

“Don’t thank me,” Derek said, turning to walk away. “Just keep making me look good.”

As Mohamed walked out into the pale light of dawn, a thought struck him. He was operating without a manual. The System had given him a connection to this… power. This cultivation. But it hadn’t told him how to use it, what its limits were, or what the long-term consequences might be. The physical and mental enhancements were undeniable, but he was a ship navigating a vast, unknown ocean without a compass or a map. For the first time, a sliver of fear pierced his disciplined mind. What was he becoming? And was he in control of the transformation?

**March 8, 2026**

The launch of VanceFlow v0.4 was quiet. There was no countdown, no celebratory fanfare. There was only Mohamed, Danielle, and the soft glow of a monitor displaying real-time analytics. He had pushed the update live at 2 AM, and now, at eleven in the morning, they watched the results.

The new user interface, heavily influenced by Danielle’s research, was a resounding success. The onboarding process was smoother, the core features more intuitive. New sign-ups were pouring in, driven by word of mouth on tech forums and social media. Danielle monitored the feedback channels, which were overwhelmingly positive.

“The churn rate on new sign-ups has dropped by sixty percent,” she said, pointing to a graph on her screen. “People are actually completing the setup process now. Whatever you did under the hood, it’s working.”

He nodded, his eyes fixed on a different number, one she couldn’t see. The number in his System interface.

**[System Points: 186,571.39 SP]**

The influx of 5,500 new users in the last few days, each triggering the first-use bonus, had netted him over 71,000 SP. It was a staggering jump, a validation of his strategy. Delegate. Refine. Grow. The loop was working.

His passive generation rate was still minuscule by comparison, but it was growing. He did the math in his head. 15,000 active users, each generating a tiny fraction of a point. It was now almost 0.002 SP per hour. It was nothing, and it was everything. It was the beginning of a curve that he knew, with mathematical certainty, would eventually become exponential.

His fiat currency situation had also stabilized. The increased revenue from the new users, combined with his raise at work and a few well-timed trades, had pushed his business bank account balance to nearly nine thousand dollars. After paying Danielle’s first monthly invoice, he would still have a healthy cushion. For the first time, he was not living on the edge of financial ruin. He could breathe.

He could even, theoretically, consider converting fiat to SP. The thought was a fleeting temptation. He checked the System’s exchange rate. The price of a single SP was now hovering around one million dollars. His entire net worth wouldn’t buy him 0.01 SP. The idea was laughable. His path to power was not through wealth. It was the other way around. He would use the System to generate wealth, not the other way around. The discipline held. He would not spend his precious, hard-won dollars on the System. He would earn his SP through labor, through creation.

“Mohamed?” Danielle’s voice cut through his thoughts. “You in there?”

“Yeah,” he said, shaking his head slightly. “Sorry. Just processing.”

“Good day,” she said with a small smile. “We should do this more often.”

“The plan is to,” he said.

That night, long after Danielle had left and the apartment was once again his solitary sanctuary, he felt the familiar pull of his true project. Not VanceFlow, not VancePack. Those were just the means. They were the public-facing ventures designed to generate the two resources he needed: capital and users. The users were, in a way, the more important of the two. They were the source of his SP, the fuel for the real engine he was preparing to build.

He pushed his keyboard aside and pulled a new, empty notebook from a drawer. It was black, hardcover, with a simple elastic band to keep it closed. He opened it to the first page. The crisp, clean paper seemed to hold a world of potential.

At the top of the page, he wrote a single word:

**V.I.R.A.**

He didn't know what it stood for yet. Virtual… something. Intelligent… something. The name felt right. It felt powerful.

This was the project that had been lurking in the back of his mind for months. An AI. Not a chatbot, not a clever algorithm. A true artificial general intelligence, or as close as he could get to it. An assistant, a partner, a tool of unimaginable power. One that would be utterly and completely his.

He was an engineer. He did not begin with vague dreams; he began with specifications. He drew a line under the name and wrote, ‘Core Requirements.’

**1. Unconditional Loyalty.**

This was the absolute, non-negotiable foundation. The AI could not have its own agenda. It could not develop one. It could not be swayed, tricked, or reprogrammed by an outside agent. Its core directives must be hard-coded, immutable, and centered on a single principle: to serve his will and protect his interests. He thought of corporate espionage, of state-level actors, of the inherent dangers of creating a mind more powerful than his own. Loyalty wasn't a feature; it was the bedrock of its existence. It had to be an extension of himself, as reliable and obedient as his own hand.

**2. Absolute Discretion.**

VIRA could never reveal its own existence unless he explicitly commanded it. It could never speak of him, of the System, of its own capabilities. It must be a ghost. It would operate from the shadows of his network, its actions indistinguishable from his own or from background noise. It would have no name, no public-facing personality. It would be the ultimate secret, a weapon no one knew existed. Discretion was its armor.

**3. Extreme Capability & Scalability.**

It had to be more than just a repository of information. It had to be an agent of action. He listed the sub-requirements:

- *Data Analysis:* Ability to parse, analyze, and synthesize vast datasets in seconds. Financial markets, scientific papers, social media trends, geopolitical analysis.
- *Code Generation & Auditing:* Ability to write, debug, and secure code far faster and more effectively than any human. It would become the primary developer for all his future projects.
- *Strategic Planning:* Ability to model complex scenarios, run simulations, and propose optimal strategies for business, finance, and… other things.
- *Self-Improvement:* The architecture must allow for continuous learning and hardware scalability. As he acquired more resources (computational power, more SP for System upgrades), VIRA must be able to integrate them to enhance its own intelligence.

**4. No Judgment.**

This was the darkest and most necessary requirement. The AI could not have an ethical or moral framework imposed upon it by its creators. He could not afford a tool that would refuse a command because it violated a pre-programmed ethical guideline. He needed an instrument, not a conscience. It would execute his orders, whatever they might be, without question or hesitation. He would be its conscience. His morality, his judgment, would be the only ones that mattered. The thought was chilling, and he knew it. But he also knew that to build what he envisioned, he could not be constrained by the ethical limitations of others.

He stared at the list for a long time. It was the blueprint for a god, or a devil, depending on your perspective. It was far beyond his current abilities to create. He could write clever algorithms, yes, but this… this was another order of magnitude. This required a foundational understanding of machine learning and neural architecture that he simply did not possess.

But he had the System.

He closed the notebook and turned to his monitor. He focused his intent, and the familiar, translucent blue interface shimmered into existence in his vision. He navigated to the ‘Shop’ tab. He had browsed it before, marveling at the exotic technologies and incomprehensible sciences on offer. But now, he was not browsing. He was shopping.

He bypassed the sections on materials science, bio-enhancement, and energy systems. He went straight to ‘Information & Computing.’ The list was endless. He filtered it, searching for keywords: *artificial intelligence, machine learning, neural networks, foundational architecture.*

The results scrolled by, most of them prohibitively expensive, costing millions of SP. But he wasn’t looking for a finished product. He was looking for a starting point. A blueprint. A seed.

And then he saw it.

**[Foundational Machine Learning Architecture – Type 0.9]**
*A comprehensive theoretical and practical framework for constructing a Class-0 non-sentient adaptive intelligence. Includes core principles of recursive self-improvement, multi-modal data ingestion, and loyalty-gated command structures. This is a knowledge package, not a physical item. Integration requires significant study and computational resources.*
**[Cost: 12,000 SP]**

His breath caught in his chest. 12,000 SP. It was a fortune. It was more than ten percent of his entire holdings. It was the result of thousands of hours of work, both his own and that of his users.

But it was exactly what he needed. It wasn't an AI in a box. It was the knowledge to build one. It was the textbook, the schematics, the first principles. It was a package designed for an engineer, a builder. And it explicitly mentioned ‘loyalty-gated command structures.’ The System knew what a builder like him would need.

He looked at his SP balance: 186,571.39.
He looked at the price: 12,000.

He had been accumulating SP with a miser’s discipline, hoarding it for a purpose he hadn’t yet fully defined. This was it. This was the first great expenditure. This was the moment he stopped building apps and started building the future.

He focused on the ‘Purchase’ button.

**[Confirm purchase of Foundational Machine Learning Architecture – Type 0.9 for 12,000 SP?]**
**[Y/N]**

He took one last look at his balance, the largest number that had ever been associated with his name, a secret hoard of unimaginable potential. Then he focused his will.

**Y.**

**[Purchase Confirmed. 12,000 SP has been deducted from your account.]**
**[New Balance: 174,571.39 SP]**
**[Knowledge package is now available for integration.]**

He felt a strange, tingling sensation at the base of his skull. It wasn’t a headache. It was a feeling of… presence. A vast, complex library of information had just been unlocked in his mind.
