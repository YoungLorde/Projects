

### **Chapter 5: First Wave**

**February 5, 2026**

The quiet of Mohamed’s apartment was a fragile thing, a thin membrane of drywall and insulation holding back the drone of Louisville. It was a quiet he cultivated, a necessary vacuum for the thoughts that swirled behind his eyes. Outside, February was a study in gray—wet asphalt, bare trees clawing at a sky the color of unpolished steel. Inside, the only light came from the twin glows of his monitors, casting long, dancing shadows across the spartan room.

He sat hunched over his keyboard, the rhythmic clatter of the keys a counterpoint to the low hum of the custom-built PC tower at his feet. It was 11:37 PM. His shift at Keen American Built had ended four hours ago, but the ghost of it clung to him: the faint, metallic scent of cutting fluid on his skin that no amount of soap could fully scrub away, the phantom vibration in his bones from standing on the concrete floor for eight hours, tending to the groaning, precise ballet of the five-axis CNC mill.

That life—the life of Mohamed Vance, machinist—felt increasingly like a costume he wore during the day. This, here, in the blue-white glare of the screen, was where the real work was done.

He ran a hand through his short, dark hair, the exhaustion a dull ache behind his temples. He toggled between his code editor and the live dashboard he’d built for his applications.

**VanceFlow Users:** 1,214
**VancePack Users:** 308
**Total Users:** 1,522

The numbers crept upward, a slow, geological process. A few new sign-ups each day, mostly from word-of-mouth in niche logistics forums or a handful of Twitter threads he’d discreetly seeded. Each new user was a small victory, a validation. More importantly, each was a tiny, microscopic tributary of a new kind of capital.

He closed his eyes and focused inward, calling up the familiar, non-visual interface of the System. It was less a screen in his mind and more a direct, intuitive knowing, like remembering his own name.

**[SYSTEM STATUS]**
**USER:** Mohamed Vance
**CULTIVATION RANK:** 0 (Passive Infusion)
**SYSTEM POINTS (SP):** 19,134.88
**FIAT ASSETS (APPROX):** $2,241.16
**SYSTEM CONNECTION:** Stable

Nineteen thousand points. It had taken him months of relentless work, of sacrificing sleep and social life, to accumulate that much. It felt like a fortune and, simultaneously, like almost nothing. He’d spent a small amount on basic knowledge packages—advanced programming languages, server architecture principles, database optimization—Type 0.0 information that was essentially a perfect, university-level education downloaded directly into his brain. It had been instrumental in building his apps. But the truly transformative items, the technologies and frameworks that hummed with the promise of a future he could barely imagine, started in the high five figures and went vertical from there.

The passive SP generation was a joke, for now. He did the math for the hundredth time, a nervous habit. 1,522 users, each a node in his burgeoning network, each contributing a quantum of their cognitive surplus, their focused attention, their problem-solving. The System harvested this infinitesimal psychic residue and converted it. The current rate was a mouthful of zeroes.

1,522 users * 0.00000013 SP/hr/user = 0.00019786 SP/hr.

About 0.0047 SP per day. It would take him over 200 days to earn a single System Point passively. He couldn't wait that long. The engine of his growth, the only thing that mattered right now, was the First-Use Bonus. Every time a new user signed up and completed their first meaningful task—charting a route in VanceFlow, organizing a pallet in VancePack—the System rewarded him. The bonus varied, seemingly based on the user's engagement and the complexity of their first task, but it averaged out to a precious 12 or 13 SP.

That was the game. Not passive income, not yet. The game was acquisition. Onboarding. Growth.

His phone buzzed on the desk beside a half-empty mug of cold coffee. A text from Danielle Jones.

*Danielle: Did you see that thread on the FreightWaves forum? Someone’s asking for a tool that does exactly what VanceFlow’s multi-stop optimizer does. Might be worth a look.*

He smiled faintly. He’d met her a month ago at a local tech meetup. She was sharp, a freelance logistics consultant who spoke in acronyms and efficiencies. She understood the real-world problems he was trying to solve with his software, the brutal, unglamorous realities of shipping and warehousing. She saw VanceFlow not as a clever piece of code but as a tool that could save a fleet manager five percent on fuel costs, which was the highest praise she could give. They’d exchanged numbers, and she’d become an informal, unpaid source of industry intelligence.

*Mohamed: Already on it. Thanks for the heads up.*

He typed the reply and put the phone down. He was playing a long game, and Danielle Jones felt like a piece he would eventually need on the board. For now, though, it was just him, the code, and the slow, agonizing climb. He cracked his knuckles, the sound loud in the silent apartment, and turned back to the screen. He had a new feature to deploy, a small tweak to the user interface that might, if he was lucky, nudge his conversion rate up by a tenth of a percent. Every little bit helped. He settled in for another three hours of work, the city outside sleeping while he laid the foundation of an empire, one line of code at a time.

**February 17, 2026**

The world changed on a Tuesday morning.

It didn't announce itself with a trumpet blast or a cataclysm. It began with a number. He was at his station at Keen, the air thick with the smell of hot metal and the deafening roar of machinery. He was halfway through a production run of aluminum transmission housings, a job that was complex enough to require his attention but repetitive enough that a sliver of his mind could wander. In a brief lull while the CNC machine was changing tools, he pulled out his phone, a habit he was supposed to have broken. He wasn't checking social media; he was checking his dashboard.

The number he expected to see was around 1,580 users. The slow, steady trickle.

The number he saw was 1,972.

He blinked, thinking it was a glitch, a caching error. He refreshed the page.

2,011.

He stared at the screen, his heart starting a low, heavy drumbeat against his ribs. That wasn't a trickle. That was a surge. In the ten minutes since his last check before clocking in, nearly two hundred people had signed up. It was impossible. He quickly navigated to his server analytics, his fingers clumsy in his haste. He scrolled down to the traffic referral sources. The vast majority of his traffic had always been direct or from a handful of forums. Now, a new entry sat at the top of the list, accounting for over ninety percent of the new traffic.

`tech-vanguard.com/reviews/vanceflow-best-logistics-tool-nobodys-talking-about`

*Tech Vanguard*. He knew the name. It wasn't one of the giants like TechCrunch or The Verge, but it was a respected, mid-tier blog with a dedicated readership of industry professionals, developers, and early adopters. The kind of people who didn't just read about new tech but actively tried it. He felt a sudden wave of dizziness, the roar of the factory floor fading into a distant hum.

He tapped the link. The page loaded slowly on the factory’s spotty Wi-Fi. The headline was there, stark and unbelievable. He scrolled, his eyes scanning the text.

"...in a market saturated with bloated, enterprise-level SaaS platforms, VanceFlow is a breath of fresh air. It is ruthlessly efficient, elegantly designed, and focuses on one thing: creating the most optimized route for complex, multi-stop deliveries. We threw our most challenging test cases at it... and it outperformed competitors costing a hundred times more..."

The review was glowing. It was detailed. The author had clearly spent days with the software, exploring every feature, every nuance. They praised the clean UI, the speed of the optimization algorithm, the sheer, unadorned utility of it.

"...Vance is not a venture-backed startup with a nine-figure valuation. As far as we can tell, it’s the work of a single, anonymous developer. There is no marketing budget, no PR team, no hype. There is only a product that works, and works brilliantly. This is the kind of software that reminds us why we fell in love with technology in the first place. It’s a tool built with purpose and precision. It’s the best logistics tool nobody’s talking about. Until now."

A klaxon blared, signaling the end of the tool change cycle. The CNC machine whirred back to life, spraying a fresh jet of coolant onto the aluminum block. Mohamed shoved his phone back into his pocket, his hands trembling slightly. He went through the motions of his job—checking tolerances with a digital caliper, logging the output, prepping the next block—but his mind was a million miles away. It was in the cloud, watching the numbers climb.

That evening, his apartment was no longer a sanctuary of quiet focus. It was a command center on the verge of being overrun. He had two terminals open. On one, he watched the server load spike, the CPU usage climbing into the red. On the other, he watched the user count.

2,500.

3,000.

3,500.

With every new sign-up, every first completed task, a notification pinged silently in his mind’s eye.

`[+13.7 SP awarded for new user activation: VanceFlow]`
`[+11.9 SP awarded for new user activation: VanceFlow]`
`[+15.1 SP awarded for new user activation: VancePack]`

It was a cascade. A deluge. The slow, patient accumulation of months was being dwarfed by the hour. He barely registered the names or the locations, just the raw, beautiful data. A trucking company in Ohio. A floral delivery service in California. A medical courier in Florida. An events company in London. The article had been syndicated, picked up by other aggregators and newsletters. The wave was building.

He frantically worked to manage the influx, spinning up new server instances on his cloud provider, optimizing database queries on the fly, patching memory leaks that hadn't been apparent at a smaller scale. This was a trial by fire. His architecture was holding, but just barely. He was a man trying to contain a tidal wave with a bucket.

He opened the System interface. The number that had been static for so long was now a blur, ticking upward so fast he could barely read it.

**SP: 28,450... 29,102... 30,051...**

He felt a frantic, giddy energy course through him, a potent cocktail of terror and exhilaration. The terror of his systems crashing, of this one golden opportunity slipping through his fingers. The exhilaration of seeing his plan, his secret, impossible ambition, actually working. The mechanism was real. It wasn't a theory anymore. This was the proof, rendered in pure, unadulterated System Points.

By 3 AM, the user count had passed 4,500. His SP balance was cresting 50,000. He was running on fumes and caffeine, his eyes burning from staring at the screen. He knew he had to be at the factory in four hours, but the thought of sleep was absurd. This was the most important moment of his life.

He watched the SP counter tick past 58,000. Then 59,000. He remembered the feeling of triumph when he’d first crossed 10,000, how it had felt like an insurmountable peak. Now, he was gaining that much in a few hours.

The sun was a dirty orange smear on the horizon when the rate of new sign-ups finally began to slow from a flood to a steady, powerful river. The first 24 hours were over. He had over 5,000 new users. His SP balance sat at a staggering 79,300. He had quadrupled his entire life’s savings of this impossible currency in a single day.

He finally stumbled away from the desk, his legs stiff, and collapsed onto his bed without changing his clothes. He fell asleep instantly, the numbers still scrolling behind his eyelids.

---

The alarm was a shriek from a distant world. Mohamed’s eyes snapped open. The light in his room was gray and unforgiving. He looked at his phone. 6:48 AM. His shift started at 7:00.

Panic, cold and sharp, cut through the fog of exhaustion. He lived a fifteen-minute drive from the factory on a good day. He launched himself out of bed, his heart hammering. He didn't have time to shower, to eat, to think. He threw on his work clothes—jeans, steel-toed boots, a clean t-shirt—grabbed his keys and wallet, and was out the door in less than ninety seconds.

He broke every speed limit on the way to Keen, his old Honda Civic groaning in protest. The clock on his dashboard was his enemy, each red digit a tick towards disaster. 6:55. 6:58. He screeched into the employee parking lot, the tires complaining, and sprinted from his car to the employee entrance, his lungs burning in the cold morning air.

He swiped his badge and burst through the door. The time clock on the wall read 7:02 AM. He was late.

Derek Holloway, his shift supervisor, was standing there, arms crossed over his barrel chest. Holloway was a man in his late fifties, with a thick mustache and hands like worn leather gloves. He’d worked at Keen for thirty years and had no patience for tardiness. He looked at Mohamed, then at the clock, then back at Mohamed. He didn’t say a word. The silence was worse than shouting.

"Sorry, Derek," Mohamed managed, his breath coming in ragged gasps. "Overslept. It won't happen again."

Holloway just grunted, a low, disapproving sound. "Your station's prepped. Get to it, Vance. That run of housings is on a tight schedule for Chrysler."

"Yes, sir."

Mohamed hurried to his station, his face burning with a mixture of shame and adrenaline. The other machinists on the floor glanced at him, some with sympathy, some with amusement. Being late was a cardinal sin in this world of tight tolerances and just-in-time manufacturing.

As he began the machine’s startup sequence, the absurdity of his situation struck him with the force of a physical blow. He was standing here, getting a silent reprimand for being two minutes late to a job that paid him $28 an hour, a job that was rapidly becoming a footnote in his life. In the last 24 hours, he had created more value—real, tangible value in the only currency that truly mattered—than this factory would generate in a month. He had a fortune sitting in a metaphysical bank account, a number that was still, even now, climbing higher and higher.

And yet, he was here. He needed this job. The $2,200 in his bank account was his safety net, his operating capital. He couldn't afford to lose this steady, reliable stream of fiat currency. Not yet. The System was his future, but the factory was his present. He had to keep the two worlds separate. He had to be more careful.

The day was a blur of forced concentration. He focused on the schematics, on the feel of the machine, on the precise measurements of each finished part. He compartmentalized, pushing the frantic energy of his other life into a locked box in the back of his mind. But every few minutes, it would rattle the cage. He’d feel a phantom buzz, imagining another notification. `[+12.4 SP]`. He’d see the user count in the reflection on the machine’s safety glass. He was living a double life, and the strain was already beginning to show.

When the final whistle blew at 3:30 PM, he felt a release of tension so profound it left him light-headed. He walked back to his car, ignoring the chatter of his coworkers, and the first thing he did was pull out his phone.

The surge had continued all day, slower than the initial frantic peak, but still powerful. Another 2,000 users had signed up while he’d been machining aluminum. He opened the System interface, his breath catching in his throat.

**SP: 104,771.21**

He read the number three times. One hundred and four thousand. He had crossed the six-figure threshold. He leaned his head back against the car’s headrest and closed his eyes. The number pulsed in the darkness. It was a weight, a responsibility, a key. It was the end of the beginning.

The drive home was slow and deliberate. He wasn't racing against the clock anymore. He was thinking. The raw excitement of the past day was beginning to cool, replaced by a cold, clear understanding.

The first-use bonuses were the rocket fuel. That was the critical insight. The passive generation, even with nearly 8,000 new users, was still negligible. It had increased, yes, but it was still a rounding error. The path to serious SP, to the kind of capital needed to buy civilization-altering technology, was not a patient wait for passive income. It was aggressive, relentless user acquisition. It was a land grab. He needed to launch more products, tap into new markets, and get as many people as possible to use his creations for the first time. The passive income was the foundation he would one day build his fortress on, but the first-use bonuses were the cannons he would use to conquer the territory.

His strategy, which had been a vague notion of "build a good product and grow," crystallized. It was now a series of concrete objectives.

1. Stabilize and scale VanceFlow.
2. Accelerate user acquisition.
3. Develop and launch a second, then a third product.
4. Repeat.

He was a good coder, but he wasn't an expert in viral marketing or network effects. And he was only one person. He couldn't manage the explosive growth of VanceFlow, provide user support, and build a new application simultaneously. He needed help.

His mind immediately went to Danielle Jones. She knew the industry, she understood the users, and she was smart. He could offload the "logistics" part of the logistics app to her—feature prioritization, user feedback, market analysis—freeing him up to focus on the core architecture and the System.

He pulled into his apartment complex, parked the car, and sat for a long moment in the quiet. The decision felt momentous. It was the first time he would be bringing an outsider into the orbit of his secret, even if she would only be seeing the outermost layer. It was a risk. But staying a one-man operation was a bigger one.

He took out his phone and typed a message.

*Mohamed: Are you available for consulting work? Paid. Logistics optimization for VanceFlow.*

He hit send before he could second-guess himself. The response came back in less than four minutes, a testament to her efficiency.

*Danielle: Define 'paid.'*

He smiled. She was direct. He considered what a freelance consultant of her caliber would charge. Probably somewhere between $100 and $150 an hour. He needed her to take this seriously. He needed to show her he wasn't just some hobbyist.

*Mohamed: $250/hr. 10 hour weekly retainer to start.*

It was a bold offer, a significant chunk of his fiat savings. But it was an investment. He was using the old world's currency to buy the time and expertise he needed to conquer the new one.

Her reply was almost instantaneous.

*Danielle: I’m listening. When can we meet?*

*Mohamed: My place. Tomorrow evening? 7 PM. I want to show you the roadmap.*

*Danielle: Send the address.*

He sent it. The deal was done. He got out of the car and walked toward his apartment, the weight of over one hundred thousand System Points a silent, thrilling burden on his soul. The game had changed.

The next evening, at 6:55 PM, Mohamed was pacing his small living room. He had cleaned meticulously, scrubbing the tiny kitchen counter, vacuuming the rug, and wiping down every surface. The place was still sparse—a couch, a coffee table, a TV he rarely used, and his workstation dominating one entire wall. It was the apartment of a man who didn't live there so much as use it for storage and sleep.

His biggest concern was the whiteboard.

It was a massive, four-by-six-foot board mounted on the wall opposite his desk, and it was covered from edge to edge in his handwriting. It was his brain, externalized. On it were diagrams, flowcharts, and equations that would make no sense to an outsider. There were user acquisition funnels and server architecture diagrams, standard stuff for a software developer. But woven through them were other, stranger things. Notations like "SP/User-Hour," "Type 0 -> Type 1 Knowledge Bridge," and "Cognitive Surplus Conversion Rate." There were complex diagrams showing the flow of data from his apps not just to a database, but to a conceptual box labeled "SYSTEM INTERFACE," with vectors pointing from it to another box labeled "KNOWLEDGE ACQUISITION."

It was a map of his entire operation, the secret and the mundane intertwined. He had considered erasing it, but it felt like a form of self-deception. This was his work. This was the truth. He decided to leave it. Her reaction would be a test.

A sharp knock on the door made him jump. He took a deep breath, smoothed down his shirt, and opened it.

Danielle Jones stood in the hallway. She was dressed in smart casual—dark jeans, a blazer over a simple top. Her hair was pulled back in a neat ponytail, and she held a leather portfolio under one arm. She had an aura of focused, professional energy that made his apartment feel even more like a monk’s cell.

"Mohamed," she said, with a crisp nod. "Thanks for having me."

"Danielle. Come on in."

She stepped inside, her eyes doing a quick, efficient scan of the room. They took in the spartan furnishings, the powerful computer rig, and then, inevitably, they landed on the whiteboard. She stopped. Her brisk, professional demeanor faltered for a fraction of a second. She walked closer to it, her head tilted.

"Can I get you something?" he asked, his voice a little tight. "Water? Coffee?"

"No, thank you," she said, her gaze still fixed on the board. She was silent for a long time, maybe thirty seconds, but it felt like an hour. He could see her mind working, trying to parse the information, to fit it into a recognizable framework. She traced one of the lines with her finger, a flowchart that started with "VanceFlow User Onboarding" and ended in the box labeled "SP/User-Hour."

Finally, she turned to face him. Her expression was unreadable, a careful neutrality.

"This," she said, gesturing to the entire board with a sweep of her hand, "is too complex for a logistics app."

It was a statement, not a question. A conclusion she had reached after processing the available data. He felt a strange sense of relief. She hadn't dismissed it as nonsense. She had recognized its complexity and judged it to be incongruous with its stated purpose.

He met her gaze. "It's not just a logistics app," he said quietly.

The words hung in the air between them. It was an admission of... something. He was offering her a glimpse behind the curtain, without showing her what was actually there. A flicker of intense curiosity crossed her face, quickly suppressed. She was a professional. She was here to do a job.

"Okay," she said, her tone shifting back to business. "Then what is it? And what do you need from me?"

She didn't press. Not yet. But he knew the question was logged in her mind, an anomaly to be investigated later. He was grateful for the reprieve.

"VanceFlow is the first step," he began, moving towards the board and deliberately pointing to the parts that made sense—the user funnels, the feature lists, the market analysis. "It's a proof of concept, and it's working better than I expected." He gestured vaguely at the user numbers he’d jotted in a corner. "We’re seeing explosive growth. I need to capitalize on it. I need your expertise to refine the product, to understand what the professional users need, to build a community around it, and to help me strategize the next features. I can handle the technical side. I need you to be the voice of the user and the expert on the industry."

He laid it all out for her: his plans for tiered pricing for enterprise clients, for an API that other companies could build on, for expanding into warehouse management and freight booking. He spoke for twenty minutes straight, outlining a vision for a comprehensive logistics ecosystem that was ambitious and, from a purely business perspective, brilliant. He was careful to frame everything in the language of market share and revenue, of fiat currency and user satisfaction. He didn't mention the System once.

She listened intently, taking notes in a crisp shorthand in a notebook from her portfolio. She asked sharp, insightful questions about his server costs, his customer support plan, his monetization strategy. She was testing him, making sure the exorbitant consulting fee he’d offered wasn't coming from a fool.

By the end of the conversation, she seemed satisfied.

"Alright, Mohamed," she said, closing her notebook. "This is ambitious. It's borderline crazy for a one-man team. But the product is solid. I've used it. The market is there. I'm in."

"Good," he said, a genuine smile breaking through his exhaustion for the first time in days. "Welcome aboard."

"First piece of advice," she said, already shifting into her role. "You need a proper company name, an LLC. 'VanceFlow' is a product name, not a corporate identity. And you need a better way to handle user feedback than just an email address."

"I'm working on it," he said.

After she left, the silence in the apartment felt different. It was no longer the silence of solitude, but the silence of anticipation. He had an ally now. A highly competent, professional ally who had no idea what game she was truly playing, but whose skills were essential to winning it.

He turned back to his computer, a new sense of purpose settling over him. The first wave had hit. He had survived. He had recruited help. Now, it was time to prepare for the next one. He had over 100,000 SP. It was burning a hole in his metaphysical pocket. It was time to invest.

He opened the System interface and navigated to the Shop. He bypassed the Type 0 knowledge, the mundane skills and information that were reflections of existing human knowledge. He filtered for Type 1.0, the very first rung of technologies that represented a true step beyond what his civilization had currently achieved. The list was short, and the prices were steep. Most were highly specific scientific principles or engineering schematics. But one item caught his eye. It wasn't a piece of hardware or a scientific theory. It was a framework. A body of knowledge.

**[Network Effect Optimization Framework]**
**TYPE:** 1.0 (Sociological Engineering)
**COST:** 8,000 SP
**DESCRIPTION:** A comprehensive framework for engineering and accelerating network effects in social and utility-based systems. Includes principles of viral loop design, incentive structuring, and memetic propagation. Integrates game theory and behavioral psychology to maximize user acquisition and retention.

It was exactly what he needed. It was the science of growth. He didn't hesitate. He focused on the purchase button, a familiar sense of intent welling up inside him.

`[Confirm purchase of Network Effect Optimization Framework for 8,000 SP?]`

He confirmed.

`[Purchase complete. 8,000 SP deducted. Beginning knowledge integration.]`

There was no flash of light, no sound. It was a feeling. A torrent of pure understanding flooded his mind, a deluge of concepts that settled into place with perfect, crystalline clarity. He suddenly *understood* virality not as a mysterious phenomenon, but as an engineering problem. He saw the mathematical underpinnings of social sharing, the precise psychological triggers that compelled a person to recommend a product to a friend. He saw how to structure a referral system not as a clumsy bribe, but as an integrated, rewarding part of the user experience. It was like a grandmaster chess player suddenly being able to see every possible move on the board, ten steps ahead.

The knowledge was more sophisticated than the Type 0 packages he'd bought before. It wasn't just data; it was wisdom. It was a new way of thinking.

He sat down at his keyboard, his fingers flying. The code he began to write was different from what he would have written an hour ago. It was more elegant, more insidious, more effective. He wasn't just adding a "share" button. He was weaving a web.

He designed a tiered referral system. When a user referred a new sign-up, both would get a month of premium features for free. If they referred three, they'd get a year. If they referred ten, they'd get a lifetime premium account. It was a simple ladder, appealing directly to the user's self-interest.

But the real genius of the framework was in the integration. He designed the prompts to appear at moments of peak user satisfaction—immediately after VanceFlow saved them an hour on a complex route, or right after VancePack perfectly optimized a truck's cargo load. The app would say, "Glad we could help! Know a colleague who could use this? Give them a free month of Premium and get one yourself." It reframed sharing not as a chore for the user, but as a gift they could give to someone else.

He spent the next two days in a feverish haze of creation, fueled by the passive energy of his cultivation and the thrill of wielding this new, potent knowledge. He slept in four-hour bursts, waking up feeling completely refreshed—a strange phenomenon he noticed but didn't have time to analyze. His body felt... more efficient. He chalked it up to adrenaline and dismissed it.

While he coded, he kept an eye on his fiat finances. His disciplined trading, combined with his spartan lifestyle, had pushed his bank account to just over $4,100. The temptation to convert it all to SP was immense. The System offered a direct conversion, but the rate was fluid, based on some complex algorithm he didn't understand. Right now, it felt low. He knew, with a certainty he couldn't explain, that a better opportunity would present itself. He had to be patient. The fiat was his war chest in the physical world, and he wouldn't spend it frivolously.

On the morning of February 20th, he deployed the new features. The referral system went live. Within an hour, he saw the first notifications. Users were taking the bait. The growth rate, which had settled into a steady rhythm after the initial blog-post-fueled explosion, began to tick upward again. It was working.

He leaned back in his chair, the first real sense of security washing over him. He had survived the first wave. He had fortified his position. He had brought in his first lieutenant. And he had forged a new weapon to accelerate his conquest.

He checked his status one last time before getting ready for his shift at Keen. The factory felt more and more like a strange dream, a life he was visiting from somewhere else.

**[SYSTEM STATUS]**
**USER:** Mohamed Vance
**CULTIVATION RANK:** 0 (Passive Infusion)
**SYSTEM POINTS (SP):** 115,312.45
**FIAT ASSETS (APPROX):** $4,188.92
**TOTAL USERS:** 9,517

The numbers were beautiful. Solid. Real. He had built something from nothing, turning lines of code into a network of human minds, and that network into a form of capital the world had never seen.

The tech blog had called him the developer of "the best logistics tool nobody's talking about." They were wrong. People were starting to talk. The network was growing. The signal was getting stronger.

He was no longer invisible.
