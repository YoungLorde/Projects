

## Chapter 4: The Girl With the Laptop Bag

**January 20, 2026**

The air in the Keen American Built factory tasted of metal and ambition. It was a flavor Mohamed Vance had come to know intimately: the sharp tang of ozone from the welders, the oily perfume of cutting fluid, the faint, chalky dust of ground steel. It was the taste of making things, of turning raw material into functional geometry. For two years, this had been the rhythm of his life, a metronome of shifts and tolerances, of physical effort that left his mind blessedly free to roam.

Today, the rhythm was different. The usual roar of the CNC mills and the percussive slam of the hydraulic presses were a familiar backdrop, but his focus was narrowed, his senses preternaturally sharp. He stood before a five-axis mill, the behemoth currently silent, its polished steel ways gleaming under the harsh fluorescent lights. In his hands was a freshly machined turbine blade, a complex, flowing shape of Inconel alloy, still warm to the touch. He ran a gloved thumb along its leading edge, feeling for imperfections the way a scholar might read braille. His eyes, however, were what registered the anomaly. A microscopic chatter mark, a faint rippling on the surface no thicker than a strand of spider silk, located on the complex curve where the blade met its root. It was almost invisible, a ghost of a flaw that would pass ninety-nine out of a hundred inspections. A month ago, he would have needed a magnifying glass and perfect lighting to spot it. Today, he saw it clear as day from arm's length.

He set the blade down on the granite inspection table, the heavy metal making a solid, definitive *thunk*. He pulled off his glasses—the cheap, wire-rimmed pair he used for close work—and rubbed the bridge of his nose. He hadn't even realized he’d put them on out of habit. He hadn’t needed them. The thought was a flicker, a momentary distraction he filed away as a fluke. Better sleep, maybe. Less screen time. A laughable notion.

"Vance."

The voice, gravelly and perpetually tired, belonged to his supervisor, Derek Holloway. Derek was a man shaped by the factory floor, with thick forearms, a slight paunch straining the fabric of his blue work shirt, and a face that seemed permanently etched with the stress of production quotas. He held a tablet in one hand, its screen glowing with spreadsheets and Gantt charts.

"Derek," Mohamed acknowledged, not taking his eyes off the flawed blade. "Tool wear on Mill Four. We're getting some harmonic vibration on the Z-axis under high load."

Derek grunted, a sound of weary confirmation. "Figured. Maintenance is already bitching about the cost of a new spindle bearing. Add it to the list." He gestured with the tablet. "Got a minute? Need to talk to you about something different."

They moved away from the noisy production area to a small, glass-walled office that overlooked the factory floor. It was Derek's cage, a place of paperwork and lukewarm coffee. The walls were covered in safety posters and production schedules.

"Look," Derek began, leaning against a filing cabinet. "You're a good machinist, Mohamed. Best I've got on second shift. You're smart, you don't cut corners, and your setups are clean. But you and I both know you're just passing through here."

Mohamed remained silent, his expression neutral. It was true. Keen American Built was a means to an end, a steady paycheck and a place to ground himself in the physical world while he built his digital one.

"Management's been breathing down my neck about scrap rates on the new aerospace contract," Derek continued, swiping through screens on his tablet. "Specifically, the castings we're getting from OmniSource. The specs are all over the place. One batch is perfect, the next is porous as a goddamn sponge. We're losing about eight percent of our raw material before it even touches a mill. It's a bloodbath."

Mohamed listened, his interest piqued. This wasn't about machining. This was about data. A complex, messy, real-world data problem.

"They want a full quality control analysis," Derek said, finally looking up. "Track every casting from receiving to final inspection. Correlate the supplier's batch numbers, the time of day they were poured, the alloy composition reports, even the goddamn humidity in the truck that delivered them. We need to find a pattern, something to take back to OmniSource to get them to tighten their process or give us a rebate."

"That's a data analyst's job," Mohamed stated simply.

"And we don't have one," Derek countered, a flicker of frustration in his eyes. "Corporate laid off the last one six months ago to 'optimize operational efficiencies.' Now they want the analysis done for free. I asked around. Your community college transcript says you aced a couple of statistics courses."

It was more than a couple. But his transcript didn't show the hundreds of hours he'd spent teaching himself Python, or the nights he'd lost building predictive models just to see if he could.

"It's more responsibility," Derek admitted, his tone shifting. He knew what was coming next. "I can't offer you a raise. Not right now. Best I can do is code it as a special project, keep you off the floor for a few hours each shift to work on it. You'll have full access to the receiving logs, the QC database, everything."

More work, same pay. For anyone else, it would be an insult. A one-way ticket to quiet resentment. But Derek wasn't offering him money. He was offering him something far more valuable.

Data.

Clean, structured, high-stakes data from a real-world supply chain. The kind of data that cost corporations millions to acquire and analyze. It was a sandbox, a living laboratory to test the theories and algorithms that were, until now, just abstract concepts in his mind. It was a direct look into the inefficiencies of the physical world, the very inefficiencies he planned to one day solve on a global scale.

"I'll do it," Mohamed said, his voice even.

Derek looked surprised, then relieved. "Yeah? Good. That's good." He handed the tablet to Mohamed. "It's all in there. The network credentials for the QC server are in the project file. Just... find something, Vance. Make me look good."

Mohamed took the tablet. The plastic casing was cool in his hands. On the screen was a portal to a river of information, a stream of numbers and records that told the story of every piece of metal that entered this building. It was a puzzle, and he was being given all the pieces. For free.

He spent the rest of his shift in a small, forgotten cubicle near the loading docks, the tablet connected to a dusty monitor. The air smelled of diesel fumes and old cardboard. He ignored it. He was already lost. He pulled down gigabytes of raw data: CSV files from the receiving department, SQL database dumps from the metrology lab, even scanned PDF invoices from the accounting system. It was a chaotic mess, a testament to disparate systems cobbled together over years.

And it was beautiful.

He began the slow, methodical work of cleaning and collating, his fingers flying across the keyboard. He wrote a simple Python script to parse the PDFs, using an open-source OCR library to extract batch numbers and dates. He cross-referenced them with the receiving logs, normalizing date formats and correcting typos. It was digital janitorial work, the unglamorous foundation of all meaningful analysis. And as he worked, he felt a deep, quiet satisfaction. This was real. This mattered. This was practice.

**January 25, 2026**

The loneliness of his ambition was a physical presence in his small apartment. It was in the silence, broken only by the hum of his computer's fans and the distant wail of a siren on the Watterson Expressway. It was in the single plate and fork he washed after a meal of rice and beans, eaten while staring at lines of code. It was in the victories no one else could see, celebrated with a quiet nod to an empty room.

Tonight was one of those victories.

He leaned back in his worn office chair, the faux leather cracking in protest. On his main monitor, a simple dashboard displayed the key metrics for VanceFlow. The number that mattered was lit in a soft, encouraging green: 1,214 active users.

It had happened faster than he'd expected. A mention on a niche project management forum, a positive review on a tech blog that catered to indie developers, and suddenly the trickle of new sign-ups had become a steady stream. Each one was a small validation, a signal that he had created something of value. But to the System, each one was much more.

He pulled up the System interface, the familiar, minimalist text overlaying his vision.

**[System Points (SP): 16,428.77]**
**[Source: First-Use Bonus (VanceFlow v0.3)]**
**[Users: 1,214]**
**[Bonus per User (Tier 2): ~13.0 SP]**

The numbers were stark and beautiful. Over nine thousand new points in just a couple of weeks. His passive generation rate was still laughably small—it would take nearly three months to generate a single point—but it was proof of concept. The true engine of growth, for now, was novelty. The first-use bonus was everything.

He looked at his bank account balance in another window: $2,247.18. The trading algorithm he’d built with his initial System knowledge was performing as expected: a quiet, methodical accumulation of small gains, compounding silently in the background. It wasn't life-changing money yet, but it was a buffer. It was freedom. It paid for his rent, his utilities, his simple meals. It paid for the time he needed to build the future.

He had two resources: time and SP. The former was finite, the latter was growing. It was time to spend some.

He closed his eyes, focusing inward, and called up the System Shop. The familiar, text-based list appeared in his mind's eye, a stark catalog of wonders.

He navigated to Computer Science. The list that unfolded was vast, a library of Alexandria for a new age. He scrolled past topics he’d considered before: advanced networking protocols, AI and machine learning fundamentals, quantum computing theory. They were all tempting, all powerful. But they were branches. He needed to build the trunk of the tree first.

VanceFlow was a success, but it was a niche product. To reach the next tier of users—tens of thousands, then hundreds of thousands—he needed something with broader appeal. Something foundational. A utility. People might not need a specialized workflow management tool, but everyone needed to manage their files.

He found the sub-category he was looking for.

**[-- Data Structures & Algorithms --]**

He scrolled down, past sorting algorithms, graph theory, and cryptographic principles. And then he saw it.

**[Advanced Data Compression Algorithms (Type 0.9 Knowledge)]**
**[Description: A comprehensive suite of theoretical and practical knowledge covering lossless and lossy compression techniques. Includes advanced implementations of Huffman coding, Lempel-Ziv variants (LZ77, LZ78, LZW), arithmetic coding, context modeling (PPM), Burrows-Wheeler transform, and novel hybrid methodologies for specific data types (text, image, audio, executable). This knowledge is approximately 50-70 years ahead of baseline 2026 Earth technology.]**
**[Cost: 1,200 SP]**

It was perfect. It was a fundamental building block of the digital world. Every file downloaded, every video streamed, every piece of software installed relied on compression. A superior compression algorithm wasn't just a product; it was a platform, a technology that could be licensed, integrated, and scaled. And at 1,200 SP, it was a bargain. It was an investment that would pay for itself many times over.

He focused on the line item, a sliver of apprehension mixing with the thrill of the decision. This was real currency he was spending, earned through hours of labor and flashes of insight.

"Purchase," he subvocalized.

**[Confirm Purchase: Advanced Data Compression Algorithms (1,200 SP)?]**
**[Y/N]**

"Yes."

There was no flash of light, no divine chorus. There was only a sudden, overwhelming pressure inside his skull, as if a dam had broken in his mind. It was not a headache. It was the opposite of pain. It was a flood of pure, structured information, a torrent of concepts and equations and logical proofs that poured into his consciousness.

He saw the elegant simplicity of Huffman trees, their branches weighted by frequency. He grasped the cleverness of the Lempel-Ziv dictionary-based approach, the way it found and replaced redundancy on the fly. He followed the mind-bending path of the Burrows-Wheeler transform, which didn't compress data itself but permuted it into a state that was *exquisitely* compressible.

But it went deeper. He saw new things, hybrid algorithms that the world had not yet conceived. A way to combine predictive modeling with context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding for text, achieving compression ratios that should have been impossible. A technique for analyzing the frequency domain of an audio file and applying a psychoacoustically-aware lossy algorithm that was indistinguishable from lossless to the human ear, at a fraction of the file size.

It was too much. His brain, his purely biological, Rank 0 brain, struggled to process the sheer volume. He slumped forward, resting his forehead on the cool wood of his desk. His heart hammered against his ribs. He breathed deeply, methodically, riding out the wave. It wasn't like learning from a book; it was like having the book's entire meaning, its every implication and connection, injected directly into his soul. The knowledge wasn't just memorized; it was *integrated*. It was part of him now, as fundamental as his knowledge of how to walk or breathe.

After ten minutes, the torrent subsided to a river, then a stream. The pressure eased. He sat up, feeling drained but exhilarated. He was a new person. He looked at the world differently. He saw the text on his screen not as letters, but as a sequence of bytes with inherent statistical redundancy. He heard the hum of his computer not as a simple noise, but as a waveform that could be analyzed and compressed.

He flexed his fingers over his keyboard. The theory was magnificent. Now came the engineering. Now, he had to build. He had to translate this divine knowledge into the clumsy, beautiful, practical language of C++.

He created a new project folder on his desktop and named it "VancePack."

**January 30, 2026**

The library was an oasis of quiet in the middle of the city's chaotic sprawl. The Iroquois branch of the Louisville Free Public Library was an old brick building, smelling of aged paper, floor wax, and the faint, ozonic scent of hardworking computer terminals. Mohamed came here once or twice a week, not for the books, but for the fiber optic connection. His apartment's internet was fine for coding and browsing, but uploading a new build of VanceFlow or downloading the massive datasets for his QC project at Keen was an exercise in frustration. Here, the gigabytes flowed like water.

He found an empty carrel in the back, near the non-fiction stacks. It was late, after ten p.m., and the library was sparsely populated. A few students hunched over textbooks, a homeless man dozing in a comfortable armchair, and a handful of people using the public computers. He settled in, plugged in his laptop, and initiated a large data transfer from Keen's servers. As the progress bar began its slow crawl, he leaned back, content to wait.

His gaze drifted across the room, over the rows of beige computer terminals. And then he saw her.

The girl from the gas station.

She was sitting three rows down, bathed in the cool, white light of a monitor. Her dark hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail, and a few stray strands had escaped to frame a face locked in fierce concentration. She wore a simple grey hoodie and jeans. Beside her on the floor was a worn, black laptop bag, the same one he remembered. She wasn't just browsing; she was working. Her fingers would fly across the keyboard in a furious burst, then she would stop, lean closer to the screen, her brow furrowed. She’d bite her lower lip, her head tilted, and then launch into another flurry of typing.

He recognized the posture of a programmer wrestling with a stubborn bug. It was a universal language of frustration and focus. He felt an odd sense of kinship. Here, in this public space, was another person living a life parallel to his own, fighting their own battles in a world of logic and syntax.

He remembered their brief, awkward conversation. Her sharp, intelligent questions about his app. He hadn't pursued it then, too focused, too wary of distractions. But seeing her now, so clearly in her element, sparked a different kind of interest. It wasn't just a passing curiosity. It was the recognition of a fellow practitioner.

The data transfer finished. He could leave. He could pack up his laptop, walk out the door, and disappear back into his solitary world. It would be the safe, easy thing to do. His path was a lonely one by necessity. The System was a secret that isolated him, a wall between him and genuine connection. Sharing his work, his real work, was impossible.

But he didn't move. He watched her for another minute as she let out a quiet, exasperated sigh and ran a hand through her hair, staring at the screen as if trying to set it on fire with her gaze.

He stood up. He slung his own laptop bag over his shoulder and walked not towards the exit, but towards her.

He stopped by her terminal, keeping a respectful distance. "Burning the midnight oil?"

She jumped slightly, startled out of her deep concentration. Her head snapped up, and her brown eyes, wide and sharp, focused on him. Recognition dawned, followed by a flicker of... something else. Not annoyance. Surprise, maybe.

"You're the gas station app guy," she said. Her voice was a little rough, as if she hadn't used it in hours.

"Mohamed Vance," he supplied, offering a small, hesitant smile. "And you're the girl with the insightful questions."

A ghost of a smile touched her lips. "Danielle Jones. And right now, I'm the girl with the buggy recursive function that's about to make me throw this computer out the window."

"Been there," he said, the words coming easily. "What's the problem?"

She hesitated, sizing him up. He wasn't pushy, just standing there, genuinely curious. It was a question any programmer might ask another. A sign of camaraderie.

"It's a freelance gig," she said, gesturing vaguely at the screen. "Logistics optimization for a local courier service. They have a fleet of five vans, and they do same-day delivery of medical supplies. The problem is routing. Every morning they get a list of a hundred or so drop-offs, each with a different package size, a delivery time window, and a priority level."

He nodded, his interest no longer feigned. This was a classic, non-trivial problem.

"I'm trying to write an algorithm that generates the most efficient routes for all five vans simultaneously," she explained, her frustration returning. "I need to minimize total drive time while respecting the delivery windows and the cargo capacity of each van. It's a variation of the Vehicle Routing Problem, a real beast."

He glanced at her screen. She was coding in Python, using what looked like a custom-built framework. He saw nested loops, conditional statements, and a function name that gave him the crucial clue: `calculate_path_permutations`.

She was trying to brute-force it. Or something close to it. She was generating all possible combinations of stops for each van and then trying to find the optimal set. For a handful of stops, that might work. For a hundred, the number of possibilities was astronomically large, greater than the number of atoms in the universe. Her computer would be calculating until the heat death of the cosmos.

She saw him looking at her code. "I know, I know," she said, a defensive edge to her voice. "It's not a true brute-force approach. I'm using some heuristics to prune the search tree, but the state space is still exploding. My function keeps hitting the recursion depth limit or just running forever. I feel like I'm trying to build a skyscraper with toothpicks."

She was smart. She knew her approach was flawed, but she was stuck in the paradigm she'd created. She was attacking the problem from the perspective of the vans.

And in that moment, Mohamed saw the entire problem laid out in his mind, not as a programmer, but as an architect. He saw the solution, elegant and efficient. It was a variation of an ant colony optimization algorithm, a concept he'd been vaguely familiar with before, but which the System had refined in his mind into a razor-sharp tool. He could give her the answer. He could sketch out the pseudocode on a napkin and save her a week of frustration.

But he didn't.

That wasn't the point. He wasn't here to show off. He was here because he had recognized something in her, and he wanted to see if he was right. He wanted to see her mind work.

He leaned a little closer, lowering his voice. "You're thinking about the vans," he said. "You're trying to find the best path for each van."

She frowned. "Well, yeah. It's a vehicle routing problem."

"What if you're asking the wrong question?" he posed gently. "What if it's not about the vans' paths? What if it's about the packages' journey?"

Danielle stared at him. The frown didn't disappear; it deepened. But it was no longer a frown of frustration. It was a frown of intense, sudden thought. He could almost see the gears turning behind her eyes, the mental models shifting and reconfiguring.

"The packages' journey," she repeated slowly, the words tasting strange in her mouth. "You mean... from the perspective of the packages?"

"Forget the vans for a second," he said, keeping his tone quiet, suggestive. "Imagine each package is a node in a graph. What are the costs of the edges between them? It's not just distance. It's time. It's the capacity it takes up. Now, what if you stop trying to build five perfect paths all at once? What if you just try to find the next single best step to take, for the entire system, at any given moment?"

He had given her nothing. No code, no algorithm name, no concrete solution. He had simply taken her problem, the one she'd been bashing her head against for days, and turned it forty-five degrees. He had offered her a new door in a wall she thought was solid.

Her eyes unfocused from his face and locked onto her screen. She wasn't seeing the code anymore. She was seeing the new structure, the new possibility. Her hands hovered over the keyboard, then came down.

She created a new file. `state_based_optimizer.py`.

He watched, fascinated. For the next ten minutes, she was a whirlwind of focused energy. She didn't speak. The only sounds were the frantic, rhythmic clatter of her keys and her quiet, sharp breaths. She wasn't just modifying her old code; she was building something entirely new from the ground up. She defined a `Package` class with attributes for location, size, and time window. She created a `SystemState` object that could represent the current location of all vans and the status of all undelivered packages.

Then she started writing the core loop. It wasn't about permutations anymore. It was a greedy algorithm, but a brilliantly clever one. At each step, it would evaluate every possible next move for every van—which package to pick up or drop off—and calculate a 'cost' for that move based on a weighted function of time, distance, and priority. It would then execute the single 'cheapest' move available across the entire system, update the state, and repeat the process.

It wasn't the full ant colony optimization he would have used, but it was a massive leap in the right direction. It was an elegant, practical solution that would reduce the problem's complexity from factorial to polynomial. It would work.

Finally, she stopped. She selected a block of code, hit the run command, and fed it a sample data set. The terminal, which had previously been hanging or crashing, spat out five neatly formatted lists of delivery stops in under three seconds.

Danielle leaned back in her chair, her whole body slumping as the tension drained out of her. She stared at the result on the screen, a look of pure, unadulterated astonishment on her face. She let out a long, slow breath that was half sigh, half laugh.

She turned to look at him. He was still standing there, his laptop bag over his shoulder, watching her. The library's quiet hum seemed to rush back in to fill the silence.

"That..." she started, then stopped, shaking her head in disbelief. "That was... wow."

"It's a good approach," he said, his voice a low murmur. "You can refine it. Add a look-ahead to avoid getting stuck in local optima, maybe introduce a stochastic element to explore different paths. But the core concept is solid."

She just stared at him, her sharp eyes searching his face. The initial surprise was fading, replaced by a keen, analytical curiosity. "I've been working on this for two weeks," she said, her voice flat with wonder. "I've read a dozen academic papers. I've tried three different approaches. You show up, ask me one question, and I solve it in ten minutes."

He just shrugged, a small, noncommittal gesture. "It was a fresh perspective. Sometimes that's all you need."

"No," she said, shaking her head again, more firmly this time. "That wasn't a fresh perspective. That was a paradigm shift. You didn't just point out a bug. You reframed the entire problem. You knew. You saw the shape of the solution instantly."

He held her gaze. The air between them was charged, not with romance, but with something purer, more electric: the thrill of mutual recognition. He saw a brilliant, tenacious mind. He hoped she saw the same.

"I've worked on similar problems," he said, which was technically true.

She wasn't buying the feigned modesty. A slow smile spread across her face. It transformed her expression from one of intense focus to one of genuine, dazzling warmth. "Right. 'Similar problems.'" She extended a hand. "Danielle Jones. It's a real pleasure to meet you, Mohamed Vance."

He shook her hand. Her grip was firm, confident. "Likewise."

The forty-five minutes he'd been standing there had felt like five. The library would be closing soon.

"I should let you get back to it," he said, taking a small step back.

"Wait," she said quickly. She pulled a pen from her bag and grabbed a stray receipt from the desk. She scribbled her name and number on it. "I feel like I owe you a coffee, or a beer, or at least a proper thank you."

"Not necessary," he said. "But..." He paused. "It would be good to have another programmer to bounce ideas off of."

It was the perfect framing. Professional. Collegial. No pressure.

"I'd like that," she said, her smile softening. She handed him the receipt.

He took it, the paper flimsy in his hand. He gave her his number in return, which she typed directly into her phone.

"Well," he said, the interaction feeling strangely complete. "Good night, Danielle."

"Good night, Mohamed."

He walked away, not looking back, though he could feel her eyes on him. He stepped out of the library's warm, quiet interior and into the cool, damp night air. The streetlights cast long, lonely shadows on the pavement. He walked to his car, the small piece of paper with her number clutched in his hand, a tangible link to the most interesting conversation he'd had in years.

He got into his car, but he didn't start the engine. He sat in the silence, the echo of their interaction replaying in his mind. The way her eyes had lit up when the solution clicked into place. The shared language they spoke without effort. It was a connection he hadn't realized he was starving for. A connection based not on his hidden, supernatural advantage, but on the part of himself that was still just him: his mind, his logic, his way of seeing the world.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. A new text message.

It was from her.

**Danielle Jones:** *Your hint was either the luckiest guess in history or you know exactly what you're doing. Which is it?*

He looked at the message, a slow smile spreading across his face. He appreciated the directness, the challenge in the question. She wasn't content with platitudes. She wanted the truth. He couldn't give her the whole truth, of course. But he could give her a piece of it.

He typed his reply.

**Mohamed Vance:** *Both.*

He watched the three little dots appear and disappear as she typed a response. A moment later, a single character popped up on his screen.

A laughing emoji. 😂

He stared at it for a long moment. It was such a simple, modern glyph, yet it felt like a significant reply. It was acceptance. It was amusement. It was the start of a new conversation.

He put his phone down on the passenger seat, the screen dark once more. He started the car and pulled out into the empty street, driving home through the sleeping city. He did not feel triumphant. He did not feel swept away. He felt a quiet, steady sense of alignment, as if a piece of his life that had been slightly askew had just clicked into its proper place.

When he got back to his apartment, the first thing he did was sit down at his computer and get back to work on VancePack. The encounter had not distracted him. It had focused him.

**February 3, 2026**

The code for VancePack came together with a speed and elegance that felt almost alien. The knowledge from the System wasn't just a textbook; it was a masterclass taught by a grandmaster. When he sat down to code, the optimal data structures and algorithms simply presented themselves. He wasn't inventing so much as transcribing, translating perfect concepts into functional C++.

He implemented a file-parsing front-end that could intelligently detect the type of data being compressed—text, image, executable, etc.—and select the best hybrid algorithm from his new arsenal. For text files, it used the predictive context-modeling technique. For executables, a variant of the Burrows-Wheeler transform that was specifically tuned for the repetitive patterns found in compiled machine code. He spent two full days crafting a user interface that was brutally minimalist: a single window where users could drag and drop files, with one button that said "Pack."

He obsessed over the details. The progress bar had to be perfectly smooth, its movement calculated based on the algorithm's progress through its phases, not just the number of bytes processed. The final output, a file with the extension `.vpack`, had to be packaged with a self-extracting header so that people who didn't have the software could still open the files. Every choice was geared towards a frictionless user experience.

During the day, he was Machinist Vance, analyzing spreadsheets for Derek Holloway. He had built a surprisingly effective model that correlated casting porosity with two unexpected variables: the ambient temperature at the OmniSource foundry (data he scraped from historical weather records) and the specific shift supervisor on duty (data he painstakingly extracted from handwritten notes on the bills of lading). His preliminary findings suggested that OmniSource's night shift, particularly on hot nights, was cutting corners on their degassing process. It was a valuable insight, and he was compiling a detailed report for Derek, but his mind was elsewhere. The supply chain data was a fascinating puzzle, but VancePack was his creation.

At night, he was the architect of his nascent empire. He packaged the first beta version of VancePack, v0.8 (he never released anything as v1.0, believing that perfection was a process, not a destination), and uploaded it to the same small tech forums and indie software repositories where VanceFlow had found its footing.

The description was simple and bold: "VancePack. A new standard in file compression. Smaller files, faster speeds. Try it." He didn't offer benchmarks or technical specifications. He let the product speak for itself.

The response was immediate and startling.

The first users were skeptical, as was the norm in these communities. They were power users, developers, and system administrators who had been using the same trusted compression tools for decades. But they were also curious.

The first comment appeared on a forum within an hour of his post.

`user_x86: Just tried this on a 1GB log file that I normally get down to ~150MB with 7z on max settings. VancePack got it down to 95MB. And it was faster. How the hell is this possible? Is this some kind of trick?`

Another followed soon after.

`dev_guru99: Okay, I'm impressed. I packed a 200MB folder of source code (mostly .cpp and .h files). WinRAR gets it to 42MB. VancePack got it to 28MB. That's not an incremental improvement, that's a leap. The author needs to publish a paper on whatever algorithm this is using.`

The System-derived knowledge was Type 0.9. It wasn't a subtle improvement. It was a generational leap, dropped into the world with no fanfare. It was like bringing a stealth bomber to a knife fight.

Within the first week, VancePack had been downloaded over 300 times. Each first-time use of his creation sent a fresh jolt of SP to his reserves.

He watched the numbers climb, the familiar thrill mixing with a growing sense of urgency.
