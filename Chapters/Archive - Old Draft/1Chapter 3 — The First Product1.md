

### **Chapter 3: The First Product**

**January 8, 2026**

The world, for Mohamed Vance, had shrunk to the twenty-seven inches of his primary monitor. Outside the thin glass of his apartment window, Louisville slept under a blanket of frigid January air. Streetlights cast cones of lonely orange onto the asphalt, and the occasional rumble of a late-night truck on the Watterson Expressway was a distant, disconnected thing. Here, in the quiet hum of his desktop computer, was the only reality that mattered.

The cursor blinked. A single, rhythmic, vertical line of white against a dark blue button that read: `SUBMIT TO PRODUCT HUNT`.

Five days. One hundred and twenty hours of his life, compressed into a sliver of time stolen from sleep, from meals, from the vacant moments between shifts at Keen American Built. He had lived on stale coffee, protein bars, and the low, thrumming energy that had been suffusing his body for weeks—an energy he still couldn't name but was beginning to trust. It was the same energy that sharpened his focus, that let him stare at dense Python libraries for hours without his eyes glazing over, that quieted the usual background noise of anxiety and doubt in his mind.

VanceFlow v0.1. It was a name born of utility, not creativity. The application itself was stark, almost brutally so. The user interface was a minimalist landscape of grey boxes, input fields, and a single, unadorned map API. There was no slick onboarding tutorial, no friendly cartoon mascot, no gamification. It was a tool, as plain and functional as a ball-peen hammer. A user—a dispatcher for a small courier service, a manager at a local distribution center—would upload a CSV file with a list of addresses and package dimensions. They would input their fleet's vehicle capacity and fuel efficiency. Then they would click `OPTIMIZE`.

Behind that simple button, Mohamed’s code would churn. It was a brute-force, greedy algorithm, stitched together from open-source libraries and his own burgeoning understanding of the Traveling Salesman Problem. It wasn't elegant. A computer science professor would likely find a dozen inefficiencies in his methods. But in his own tests, using publicly available logistics data sets, it worked. It produced routes that were, on average, four to seven percent more efficient than the routes generated by the free tools most small businesses used. It saved a little time, a little fuel. It created a tiny sliver of value.

Was it enough?

The System’s message from weeks ago was seared into his memory, a permanent fixture in his mind's eye. `Value is the currency of the universe. Create it. Capture it. Convert it.` He had captured nothing yet. His SP balance, displayed in the barely-there, translucent overlay only he could see, read a pathetic `0.7`. A remnant of his initial stock trading, a microscopic rounding error in the grand scheme of things. His fiat bank account, after a few more cautiously successful trades, hovered around eight hundred dollars. He was a man standing at the shore of an ocean with a teaspoon.

This was the first cast of a real net. Vance Digital LLC was a legitimate, registered entity. The domain was live. The server, a cheap virtual private server rented from a company in Germany, was running. Everything was in place. All that was left was the click.

He took a deep breath, the air in the small apartment feeling thin and used. He had written the copy for the Product Hunt submission and the posts for the three developer forums—one for Python enthusiasts, one for logistics software specialists, and a general startup forum. The messaging was simple: *“I’m a machinist who taught myself to code. I built a free tool to help small businesses optimize their delivery routes. Looking for beta testers to give feedback.”* It was honest. It framed him as an underdog, which he was. It lowered expectations, which was wise.

He moved the mouse. The cursor slid over the button. His finger twitched on the left mouse button. This was it. The proof. Either the System was a delusion, a fantastically detailed hallucination brought on by stress or a brain tumor, or it was the fundamental reality he now believed it to be. If it was real, then creating value—even this small, unrefined sliver of it—had to be rewarded. It was the core thesis of his new life.

He clicked.

The page refreshed. A confirmation message appeared. *“Your product has been submitted.”*

He did the same on the three forums, copying and pasting his carefully crafted text, attaching a single, clean screenshot of the UI. Then he pushed his chair back, the wheels squeaking in the profound silence of the room.

There was nothing left to do but wait.

The silence that followed was heavier than before. It was the silence of a held breath, of a dropped stone waiting to hit the bottom of a well. He stood up and walked to the window, peering down at the empty street. The world looked exactly the same. No flash of light, no cosmic hum, no sign that the rules of the universe had just been tested. There was only the cold, the dark, and the quiet.

He felt a sudden, sharp pang of doubt. What if this was all nonsense? What if he’d just spent five days of sleepless, caffeine-fueled mania building a mediocre web app that no one would ever use? What if he was just a machinist with delusions of grandeur, chasing a ghost in his own head?

He looked at his hands. The calluses were still there, rough and familiar from years of handling steel and aluminum. But the fine tremors that used to plague him after a long day of operating the CNC machines were gone. His grip felt stronger, more certain. He could feel the blood pulsing in his fingertips with a clarity he’d never had before. Something was happening to his body, a slow, passive refinement that was undeniably real. If that was real, the rest had to be.

He returned to the desk, opened a fresh tab, and began to read the documentation for a more advanced geospatial library. The waiting would be torture. The only antidote was work.

---

The first twenty-four hours were a slow drip of validation. He woke up after four hours of deep, dreamless sleep—another new development, his body now seemed to require less rest but made brutally efficient use of what it got—to find seven new sign-ups. Seven email addresses in his database. Seven people who had seen his post and thought, *“Maybe this is useful.”*

He felt a surge of something warm and unfamiliar. It wasn't just relief; it was a craftsman's pride. He had made a thing, and other people were choosing to interact with it. He checked his SP balance. Still `0.7`.

He pushed down the disappointment. The System hadn’t specified the exact mechanism. Maybe it required a critical mass. Maybe it paid out on a weekly basis. Maybe the value created by seven beta users for a free tool was so infinitesimally small that it didn't even register.

He went to work at Keen American Built. The factory was an orchestra of controlled violence. The percussive clang of the stamping press, the high-pitched scream of carbide end mills cutting through steel, the hydraulic hiss of the press brakes. The air was thick with the smell of cutting fluid, a smell like hot metal and sulfur that clung to his clothes and hair.

For the first time, he saw the place not as a collection of machines and tasks, but as a system. A logistics problem of its own. He watched as a forklift driver, a grizzled man named Sal, drove a pallet of raw aluminum stock from the receiving bay to the staging area. Sal had to wait for two minutes while another forklift moved a bin of finished parts out of the main aisle. Two minutes of wasted time. Two minutes of a running engine, burning propane. Two minutes of a salaried employee being paid to sit still.

He saw the flow. Raw materials came in, were moved to storage, then to a specific machine, then to finishing, then to quality control, then to packaging, then to the shipping dock. It was a river of physical objects, and at every bend, at every confluence, there were eddies of inefficiency. A machinist waiting for a specific tool. A batch of parts sitting for an hour because the QC inspector was on break. A shipment delayed because the paperwork wasn't ready.

He wasn’t thinking about how to help Keen. Mr. Keen, a third-generation owner who saw his employees as interchangeable, expensive parts, had earned none of his loyalty. Instead, Mohamed was studying it. This factory was a living, breathing data set. A complex, multi-variable problem he could observe in real time. He filed the observations away, mental notes for a future, more powerful version of VanceFlow. One that didn't just optimize routes outside the building, but inside it as well.

During his lunch break, he checked his phone. Twenty-three users. The post on the logistics forum had gained some traction. A few comments. *“UI is clean. Simple. I like it.” “Ran a test route for my 15-stop run tomorrow. Shaved 11 minutes off the Google Maps estimate. Not bad.” “Is there a way to specify different vehicle types in a single fleet?”*

The feedback was a jolt of electricity. He quickly typed a reply to the last comment. *“Not yet, but multi-vehicle support is top of the list for v0.2. Thanks for testing it out.”*

He checked his SP. Still `0.7`. The doubt began to creep back in, a cold tide at the edge of his confidence. He had created measurable value. Eleven minutes saved for one user. That wasn’t nothing. Why wasn’t the System reacting? Was the threshold higher than he thought? Did users have to *pay* for it to count as true value creation? The thought sent a chill through him. If the System only rewarded monetary transactions, his path just became infinitely harder.

He finished his shift, his movements precise and economical. He calibrated a five-axis CNC mill, his hands steady as a surgeon's as he seated a new block of titanium into the chuck. The machine’s tolerances were measured in ten-thousandths of an inch, and for the first time, he felt like his own senses were operating at a similar level of precision. He could feel the subtle vibration of the machine through the concrete floor, hear the slight change in pitch as the cutter engaged the metal, a sign that the feed rate was perfect. His body was becoming a finely tuned sensor, an extension of the machine itself.

He drove home, the nagging anxiety about the System a knot in his stomach. He had to be patient. This was a marathon, not a sprint. But the lack of any response felt like a silent refutation of his entire hypothesis.

He parked his decade-old Civic, walked up the three flights of stairs to his apartment, and unlocked the door. The first thing he saw was the glow of his monitor, which he’d left on. He dropped his keys in the bowl by the door, kicked off his work boots, and walked into the living room.

He glanced at his user dashboard. The number was `100`.

It had happened in the last hour of his shift. A popular tech newsletter had mentioned his app in their daily roundup. *“A machinist from Kentucky built a surprisingly effective free logistics tool…”* That small mention had sent a wave of new users to the site. One hundred. A clean, psychologically significant number.

He felt a small thrill. One hundred people were using his code. But the thrill was immediately followed by the familiar dread. He braced himself for disappointment and looked at the top right of his vision, where the System interface hovered.

His SP balance read `0.7`.

The knot in his stomach tightened into a cold, heavy stone. It was over. He was wrong. The whole thing was a lie, a delusion. The passive physical enhancements were real, but the economic engine, the part he was banking his entire future on, was a fantasy. He had created value. He had built a tool, and a hundred people were using it. The System had done nothing.

He sank into his desk chair, the cheap pleather groaning under his weight. He stared at the screen, at the list of anonymous email addresses in his database, and felt a profound, hollow emptiness. All that effort. All that hope. For nothing. He felt like a fool.

And then it happened.

The number `0.7` flickered.

He blinked, thinking it was a trick of the light, of tired eyes. It flickered again. It wasn’t a change. It was more like a digital stutter, a system preparing to update.

A new line of text materialized below his balance, written in the same minimalist, impossibly crisp font of the System.

`[First-Use Value Creation Event Triggered: User Threshold Reached (Software - Logistics)]`
`[Calculating… ]`

Mohamed stopped breathing. His heart, which had been sinking into a pit of despair, suddenly hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird.

`[User #24: Value Signature Confirmed. Applying First-Use Bonus: +14.2 SP]`

The number `0.7` instantly changed. `14.9`.

Before he could process it, another line appeared.

`[User #25: Value Signature Confirmed. Applying First-Use Bonus: +11.8 SP]`

`14.9` became `26.7`.

`[User #26: Value Signature Confirmed. Applying First-Use Bonus: +16.1 SP]`

`26.7` became `42.8`.

It was like watching a slot machine, but the numbers only went up. The System was working backward, processing the queue of users who had signed up before the hundred-user threshold was met. Each new line was a confirmation, a validation, a deposit of cosmic currency directly into his account. The notifications began to scroll faster and faster, a waterfall of text.

`[…Applying First-Use Bonus: +13.5 SP]`
`[…Applying First-Use Bonus: +10.9 SP]`
`[…Applying First-Use Bonus: +17.0 SP]`

The numbers on his balance blurred into a rising tide. `157.4`… `289.1`… `455.9`… `781.2`…

He felt light-headed. He gripped the arms of his chair, his knuckles white. The quiet hum of his computer seemed to fade, replaced by the roaring in his ears. This wasn't a trickle. This was a flood. The System wasn't rewarding him with fractions of a point. It was a deluge. The bonus for each user seemed to vary, some as low as ten, one as high as seventeen. He didn’t understand the variation—perhaps it was tied to the potential value of the business they represented?—but it didn't matter. The average was holding strong.

The scroll of notifications continued, a relentless, silent cascade. The final user, number one hundred, was processed.

`[User #100: Value Signature Confirmed. Applying First-Use Bonus: +12.7 SP]`

The final number settled.

`SP Balance: 1,312.7`

Mohamed stared. He stared for a full minute, his mind a complete blank. Then, a wave of vertigo washed over him. The room tilted. He felt his legs go weak, and he had to physically push himself deeper into his chair to keep from sliding onto the floor. He took a gasping breath, then another.

One thousand, three hundred and twelve point seven System Points.

Yesterday, he had less than one. He was a machinist with a hobby project and a wild, unproven theory. Today… today he was something else. He didn’t know what. But he knew, with a certainty that vibrated in his very bones, that everything had just changed.

A final notification appeared, smaller, quieter, almost an afterthought.

`[Passive Value Generation Initiated. Rate: 0.000013 SP/hr]`
`[Rate determined by active user base and sustained value signature.]`
`[Compounding… ]`

He almost laughed. The passive rate was laughably small. A meaningless string of zeroes. It would take him over a year to accumulate a single SP at that rate. But it wasn't meaningless. It was proof of a second mechanism. The passive income was the long game, the slow accumulation.

But the first-use bonus… that was the weapon. That was the engine of growth.

He scrambled for the worn notebook and pen he kept on his desk. His hand was shaking, but he forced it steady. He wrote, the words coming in a rush, his thoughts crystallizing on the page.

*January 10, 2026*

*It works. Threshold is 100 users for this category (Software). The System back-pays for all users once the threshold is hit.*

*Two mechanisms confirmed:*

*1. First-Use Bonus: One-time payment per new user. Seems to average around 13 SP. This is the primary driver. This is the accelerator.*

*2. Passive Generation: Continuous, based on active users. Current rate is negligible (0.000013/hr). Might increase if users get more value out of the product (e.g., paid features, more frequent use).*

He underlined the next sentence twice.

*The passive rate is nothing for now. But the first-use bonus is the weapon. The path forward is not deep engagement with a few users. It is massive, rapid acquisition of new users. More users = more first-use SP. Scale is everything.*

He looked at his new balance. `1,312.7 SP`. It was a fortune, and it was nothing. He instinctively called up the conversion menu in the System interface. The option to convert SP to fiat currency was still there, a tantalizing, impossible dream.

`[Convert SP to Fiat Currency (USD)]`
`[Current Rate: 1 SP = $1,000,000.00 USD]`
`[Note: High initial conversion cost reflects capital seeding phase. Rate subject to change based on System maturity.]`

He couldn't even convert two SP. The idea was absurd. But now he understood. The System wasn't designed for him to cash out small amounts. The conversion rate was a barrier to prevent exactly that. It was an end-game goal, a way to manifest massive amounts of capital once he had already built a galactic-scale value engine.

The first-use bonuses were different. They weren't a conversion. They were a direct reward. A grant. Seed funding from the universe itself. The System wasn't just a marketplace; it was an incubator. It was giving him the resources he needed to build the very thing it wanted him to build: a value-generating enterprise.

He had capital now. Not fiat, but something far more valuable. He had SP. And with SP, he could buy tools.

He closed his notebook and opened the System’s `[SHOP]` interface. The last time he’d looked, it was an act of fantasy, like a child browsing a toy catalog he could never afford. Now, he was a buyer.

The categories were still overwhelming. `[Physics]`, `[Biology]`, `[Cognitive Science]`, `[Engineering]`, `[Art & Culture]`. Most of the items were priced in the millions or billions of SP. `[Theory of Unified Spacetime]`, `[Blueprint: Self-Replicating Nanoforge]`. He ignored them. He used the search function, his fingers flying across the keyboard.

`Search: Logistics`

The results populated instantly. Most were still wildly out of his price range. `[Applied Gravitonics for Mass Transit - 7.5M SP]`, `[Predictive Fleet Consciousness AI Core - 1.2M SP]`. But he filtered by price, lowest to highest. And there, near the bottom, he found something.

`[Item: Supply Chain Optimization Theory, Vol. 2]`
`[Origin: K'tharr Mercantile Combine (Extinct)]`
`[Type: 0.8 Civilization Knowledge (Theoretical)]`
`[Description: A foundational text on multi-dimensional resource flow and probabilistic bottleneck analysis. Focuses on optimizing complex, non-linear supply chains with dozens of independent variables. Assumes sub-light travel and basic computational infrastructure. Considered rudimentary but effective.]`
`[Price: 500 SP]`

His breath caught in his throat. It was perfect. It wasn't impossibly advanced. It was near-human, Type 0.8, which was why the price was so low. It was theory, not a finished blueprint, which meant it required effort to implement. It was exactly what he needed. It was the next step up from the brute-force algorithms he was currently using. *Probabilistic bottleneck analysis.* The phrase alone made his mind race.

He had `1,312.7 SP`. The purchase would take a significant chunk of his newfound wealth. It was a risk. But it wasn't a gamble. It was an investment. An investment in the product, which was his only source of income.

He focused on the `[PURCHASE]` button next to the item description. He hesitated for only a second, then committed.

A confirmation dialog appeared. `[Confirm purchase of "Supply Chain Optimization Theory, Vol. 2" for 500 SP?]`

He selected `[YES]`.

His SP balance instantly dropped to `812.7`.

For a moment, nothing happened. He felt a flicker of panic. Had he been scammed by a cosmic ghost? Then, a sensation bloomed in the center of his mind. It wasn't a sound or a light. It was a feeling of… structure. It was as if a vast, complex, and perfectly organized library had been constructed in an empty wing of his brain in the span of a single heartbeat.

He didn't see equations or text. He *understood* them. Concepts that would have taken a PhD mathematician years to master were suddenly intuitive, self-evident. He understood how to model a supply chain not as a simple line, but as a multi-layered graph. He understood how to assign probabilities to potential delays—a truck getting a flat tire, a supplier being late with a shipment—and factor those probabilities into the overall optimization. He understood how to treat time, fuel, vehicle wear, and driver salary not as separate variables to be minimized one by one, but as interconnected elements of a single, fluid cost-surface that could be shaped and flattened.

It was like he’d spent his life trying to understand architecture by looking at a single brick, and someone had just handed him the complete blueprints for a cathedral. He saw the elegant, brutal logic of the K'tharr Mercantile Combine. They were traders, merchants, and their mathematics was purpose-built for one thing: efficiency.

He sat motionless for ten minutes, his eyes closed, simply exploring the new knowledge in his head. He could see the code he needed to write. He could see the flaws in his current algorithm, the embarrassing simplicity of it. He felt a blush of shame, followed by a surge of fierce, focused ambition.

He could make VanceFlow better. Not just incrementally better. An order of magnitude better.

But first, there was a practical matter. The feedback on the forums had been overwhelmingly positive, but one user had pointed out a glaring UI bug. When a user uploaded a CSV with an incorrectly formatted address, the application didn't just reject the single line item; it crashed the entire session, forcing the user to start over. It was a sloppy, amateur mistake. It was costing him users.

He could spend a day fixing it himself. Or…

He logged into his fiat bank account: `$817.34`. He logged into a popular freelancing website, posted a job—*“Python/Flask Developer Needed for Urgent Bug Fix”*—and described the problem in precise detail. Within twenty minutes, he had a dozen applicants. He chose one with stellar reviews, a developer from Eastern Europe who called himself “CodeWraith.” They agreed on a price: two hundred dollars.

Mohamed transferred the money. It was almost a quarter of his liquid cash. A week ago, spending that much on a single bug fix would have been unthinkable. Now, it was just a business expense. A necessary grease for the wheels of the machine he was building. Fiat was a tool, just like SP. It was to be used, not hoarded. While CodeWraith fixed the front end, he could focus on the back end. On rebuilding his entire optimization engine with alien mathematics.

His bank account was now just over $600. His SP balance was `812.7`. And his to-do list was clear.

**January 12, 2026**

The factory floor at Keen American Built had become his laboratory. The K'tharr knowledge framework acted like a new filter on his vision. When Sal the forklift driver had to wait for a clear aisle, Mohamed’s mind automatically began to model the event.

*Event: Aisle Blockage. Duration: 1m 47s. Root Cause: Sub-optimal scheduling of material transit. Node P17 (Stamping Press Output) and Node R3 (Raw Material Input) requested aisle access simultaneously. Priority system is informal (whoever gets there first). This is a probabilistic bottleneck. Probability of occurrence: 12% during peak hours. Compounded cost per year: ~$45,000 in wasted labor and fuel.*

He could see it all. He could see the invisible lines of connection, the flow of cause and effect. He saw the factory not as a physical space, but as a living algorithm, and it was running inefficiently. He felt a strange sense of detachment, like a doctor diagnosing a patient he had no intention of treating. This knowledge wasn't for Keen. It was for him. It was practice.

CodeWraith delivered the bug fix in less than three hours. The code was clean, efficient, and perfectly documented. Mohamed tested it, confirmed it worked, and pushed the update live. He sent the freelancer a fifty-dollar bonus and a five-star review. The entire transaction had taken less time than his lunch break. Efficiency. This was how it was supposed to work.

His nights were a blur of furious creation. He wasn't just coding; he was translating. He was taking the abstract, hyper-logical principles of the K'tharr and figuring out how to express them in Python. He built a new module from scratch, one that created a multi-dimensional cost-surface for every potential route. He integrated probabilistic modeling for traffic, weather, and even vehicle breakdown, pulling data from public APIs.

He would work until his body screamed for rest, then fall into a deep, dreamless sleep for three or four hours, and wake up with the next section of the algorithm already clear in his mind. The passive physical refinement was accelerating. He felt stronger, sharper. A heavy wrench that used to strain his wrist now felt light as a toy. His reaction times were faster. He once fumbled a delicate calibration tool, and his other hand shot out and snatched it from the air before his conscious mind had even registered the drop.

He didn't understand the source of the physical changes, but he stopped questioning it. It was part of the System. His body was the hardware, the System was the operating system, and his mind was the user. The hardware was simply being upgraded to better run the software.

By January 15th, VanceFlow v0.2 was ready. He ran his benchmark tests. The old version, v0.1, had managed a 4-7% efficiency gain. The new version, powered by the K'tharr theory, was consistently hitting 12-19%. It was a staggering leap in performance. It could route a fleet of twenty vehicles with fifty stops each in under a second, providing not just one optimal route, but several alternatives based on different risk tolerances.

He pushed the update live and posted a short announcement on the forums. *“VanceFlow v0.2 is live. The routing algorithm has been completely rebuilt for higher accuracy and speed. Multi-vehicle support is still in the works. As always, feedback is welcome.”*

The effect was immediate. His user base had grown steadily over the past few days, ticking up to around 250 as word of mouth spread. Each new user was a small, satisfying chime of SP in his account. His balance had climbed back over 2,000.

With the new version, the growth curve steepened. Users on the forums started posting their results. *“Holy crap. v0.2 just saved my ass. A last-minute 30-package run that usually takes me 4 hours, it routed in a way that’ll get it done in 3. I don’t know how it works, but it does.”* Another: *“The new algorithm is legit. I compared its output to our $5,000/year commercial software, and VanceFlow’s route was 8% cheaper on fuel. For a free tool, that’s insane.”*

The praise was gratifying, but Mohamed focused on the data. New sign-ups. The number on his dashboard began to climb faster. 300. 350. 400. Each one was a deposit. `+11.4 SP`. `+15.2 SP`. `+13.9 SP`. The numbers were a comforting, steady rhythm, the heartbeat of his growing enterprise.

His small, cautious stock trading was also paying off. He’d invested his remaining fiat into a few biotech and semiconductor stocks, and a lucky break had nearly doubled his money. His bank account was back up to around $1,100. It felt trivial compared to his SP wealth, but it was necessary. Fiat was the currency of the old world, and he still had to live in it.

**January 18, 2026**

The coffee at the gas station near the factory was terrible, a bitter, burnt sludge that he diluted with two packets of sugar and a splash of creamer. But it was hot, and it was convenient. He was leaning against the counter, scrolling through user feedback on his phone, when a voice cut through his concentration.

“VanceFlow?”

He looked up. A woman was standing there, holding a bottle of water and a bag of pretzels. She was maybe his age, dressed in practical jeans and a dark grey hoodie. Her dark hair was pulled back in a simple ponytail, and her eyes were sharp, intelligent, and focused directly on him. There was a smudge of what looked like grease on her cheek.

Mohamed’s guard went up instantly. No one knew he was behind VanceFlow. He used a pseudonym on the forums, and the LLC registration was just an address.

“I’m sorry?” he said, his voice neutral.

“The app,” she said, nodding toward his phone. “My cousin runs a small flower delivery business. He won’t shut up about it. Says it’s saving him a couple hundred bucks a week on gas. I saw the logo on your screen.”

He glanced down. He’d left the app’s simple dashboard open. A rookie mistake. He mentally kicked himself.

“It’s just a side project,” he said, pocketing the phone. He kept his tone dismissive, disinterested.

“Side project?” She raised an eyebrow. She had a directness that was slightly unnerving. “The jump from the first version to the second was more than a side project. The pathing logic is completely different. You didn’t just iterate; you rebuilt the core engine. Did you switch from a greedy algorithm to something like a multi-agent reinforcement model? Or is it a custom heuristic? Because the solutions it’s spitting out are too elegant for a standard A-star search.”

Mohamed’s carefully constructed nonchalance evaporated. She wasn’t just a casual user. She was speaking his language. She saw the architecture behind the interface. He stared at her, his mind racing. Who was she?

“It’s a hybrid approach,” he said, the words coming out before he could stop them. It was a reflex, the engineer in him unable to resist a technical discussion.

“Hybrid,” she repeated, nodding slowly, as if the word confirmed a hypothesis. “Makes sense. That’s how you’d get the speed without sacrificing accuracy.” She paid for her items, the cashier barely registering their conversation. She turned back to him, her expression unreadable. “You’re the machinist from the forums, aren’t you? The one who built it.” It wasn’t a question.

He didn’t answer. He just held her gaze. Admitting it felt dangerous, like revealing a critical secret.

She gave a small, wry smile. “Don’t worry. I’m not going to blow up your spot. I’m just… impressed. It’s a clean piece of work.” She paused, then added, “My name’s Danielle Jones.”

She offered no other information. Not where she worked, or how she knew so much about algorithms. She just let her name hang in the air.

“Mohamed,” he said, after a beat. He didn’t offer his last name.

“Well, Mohamed,” she said, her smile not quite reaching her eyes. “Keep up the good work.”

She turned and walked out, the bell on the gas station door jingling softly. He watched her go, a strange mix of paranoia and intrigue churning in his gut. She got into a beat-up Ford Ranger, the kind of truck a contractor or an engineer might drive, and pulled out onto the road.

He stood there for a full three minutes, replaying the six sentences they’d exchanged. She was sharp. Too sharp. She had dissected his work from the outside, with startling accuracy. He felt exposed. His secret, the foundation of his entire new life, had been glimpsed by a complete stranger over a cup of bad coffee.

Then, he deliberately shut it down. He couldn’t afford the distraction. She was an anomaly, a random variable. He had a plan to follow, a system to build. He threw the rest of his coffee in the trash, the bitter taste a fitting match for his unease, and headed back to the factory.

**January 20, 2026**

The whiteboard in his apartment was his sanctuary, his strategic map. It was covered in diagrams, equations, and lists. He spent a few minutes erasing old metrics and goals, the squeak of the marker on the smooth surface a satisfying sound of progress.

He looked at the numbers. They were solid. They were real.

`User Base: 512`

The number had crossed the 500 mark an hour ago. The flow of first-use bonuses had been steady, a constant affirmation. He brought up the System interface, the translucent text hovering over the physical whiteboard.

`SP Balance: 7,341.9`

Seven thousand. In less than two weeks, he had accumulated more of the universe’s primary currency than he could have imagined. The passive rate was still tiny, but it was growing in lockstep with his user base.

`Passive Value Generation: 0.00006656 SP/hr`

He wrote the new numbers on the board. His fiat account, buoyed by another successful trade, was now close to fourteen hundred dollars. Enough for another freelancer, or a server upgrade, or just to live on for a month if he quit his job. The thought was tempting, but he dismissed it.
