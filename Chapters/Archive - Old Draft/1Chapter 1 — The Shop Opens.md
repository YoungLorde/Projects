

The shriek of the five-axis mill biting into a block of 7075 aluminum was a constant, high-pitched sermon on the nature of force and matter. For eight hours a night, five nights a week, Mohamed Vance was a high priest of this sermon, tending to the machine’s needs, monitoring its vital signs, and ensuring its communion with the raw material resulted in a perfect, gleaming component for a client he’d never meet. The air in the cavernous main floor of Keen American Built was thick with the metallic tang of atomized coolant, a scent that clung to his clothes and lived in the back of his throat long after his shift ended. It was the smell of making things, the smell of a paycheck. It was the smell of a life stalled in neutral.

He moved with an economy of motion that bordered on languid, yet every action was precise. His long, lean frame, draped in a gray, oil-stained jumpsuit, navigated the concrete floor with the quiet confidence of a man who knew every crack and seam. He checked the tolerances on the piece currently being machined, his dark eyes scanning the digital readout. Plus or minus five ten-thousandths of an inch. A human hair was a clumsy giant by comparison. The machine, a German-made behemoth that cost more than the houses in his neighborhood combined, was holding the line perfectly. He made a note on the digital log, his fingers tapping the hardened glass of the control panel with practiced familiarity.

The clock on the wall, a simple red LED display, read 11:58 PM. December 31st, 2025. Outside, Louisville was preparing to welcome the new year with fireworks and drunken revelry. Inside Keen American Built, the only celebration was the steady, thrumming rhythm of industry. A few of the other guys on the graveyard shift had brought in cheap party hats, their conical shapes looking absurd atop heads already covered in safety-mandated beanies. Dale, a thick-set man with a graying beard who ran the lathes two rows over, had been making jokes all night about his resolution to win the lottery and buy this place just to fire their supervisor. Mohamed offered a polite, thin smile when required but otherwise remained insulated in his own thoughts.

His thoughts were, as always, a quiet ledger of his current state. Checking account: $847.32. Rent due in four days. Student loan payments, deferred but looming like a patient predator, totaled just over sixty-three thousand dollars. An education he hadn’t even finished. He’d been a top student in the University of Louisville’s engineering program, the kind of mind that saw the elegant mathematics behind the physical world as clearly as others saw the words on a page. But the lectures had felt like listening to a child explain the alphabet. The required courses outside his focus were an intolerable waste of time and money. His attendance had plummeted, his professors had grown frustrated with his dismissive brilliance, and the inevitable expulsion had been less a tragedy and more a quiet, mutual agreement that he didn't belong there. So now he was here, babysitting a machine that performed the elegant mathematics for him. The irony was a dull, constant ache.

He wasn’t bitter. Bitterness was an inefficient emotion. He was simply… assessing. He was a system running on suboptimal hardware, in a suboptimal environment. The problem was complex, the variables numerous. The solution remained elusive.

The final seconds of the year bled away. Dale let out a half-hearted “Woo!” that was mostly swallowed by the din of the machinery. The clock ticked over. 12:00 AM. Then 12:01 AM. January 1st, 2026.

And then, something flickered in his vision.

It was not a flash of light. It was a change in the quality of the light, a subtle imposition of order on the chaos of photons. In the center of his view, superimposed over the humming CNC machine, a box of translucent, pale blue light coalesced. It was perfectly stable, its edges sharp and defined, its contents written in a clean, sans-serif font that seemed to radiate a soft, internal luminescence.

[System Initialized]
[Host Designation: Mohamed Vance]
[Status: Rank 0]
[System Points (SP): 1.0]

Mohamed didn’t flinch. He didn’t gasp or rub his eyes. His first thought was not of magic or divine intervention. It was of pathology. *Aneurysm,* he thought, the word clinical and detached. *Or perhaps a retinal detachment. Or a neurological event triggered by solvent fumes.* He took a slow, deliberate breath, inhaling the familiar coolant-laced air, and held it for five seconds. He cataloged his physical sensations. No headache. No dizziness. No numbness in his limbs. His heart rate, which he could feel as a steady, slow thump in his chest, remained unchanged.

He closed his eyes. The blue box was still there, projected against the blackness of his eyelids. It was not an external phenomenon. It was *in* his perception.

He opened his eyes. The box remained, hovering in space about six feet from his face. He turned his head. The box stayed in the center of his vision, moving with his gaze. He looked up at the high, sodium-vapor lights of the factory ceiling. The box followed. He looked down at the scuffed toes of his work boots. The box was there.

This was not a simple hallucination. Hallucinations, especially those induced by fatigue or chemicals, tended to shimmer, to distort. This was as crisp and stable as the machine’s digital display. It had the undeniable solidity of data.

He reached out a hand, slowly, and tried to touch it. His fingers passed through the light without resistance. There was no tactile sensation, no change in temperature. It was pure information, displayed directly into his visual cortex.

He focused on the text. [Host Designation: Mohamed Vance]. It knew his name. That was… unsettling. It implied a level of surveillance that was either governmental or something far beyond it. [Status: Rank 0]. A classification. A starting point. [System Points (SP): 1.0]. A currency.

Beneath the initial lines, another line of text appeared, as if prompted by his focus.

[Open User Agreement?]

A simple binary choice. He thought, *Yes.*

The box expanded, the text scrolling into view at a comfortable reading speed. It was dense, legalistic, and utterly fascinating. He stood there for what must have been ten minutes, oblivious to the factory around him, the shriek of the mill fading into a background hum as he absorbed the rules of a new reality.

**USER AGREEMENT & SYSTEM PRIMER**

**1. Nature of the System:** The System is a multiversal framework designed to facilitate the growth and development of sentient entities. Its primary function is to provide access to knowledge and technologies from disparate civilizations.

**2. Host Exclusivity:** The System interface is keyed to the Host’s unique quantum signature. It is invisible and inaccessible to all other entities. Disclosure of the System’s existence is a violation of this agreement and may result in punishment.

**3. System Points (SP):** SP is the primary currency for all transactions within the System.
    *   **3a. Seed Grant:** A one-time grant of 1.0 SP is provided upon initialization.
    *   **3b. Fiat Conversion:** Terrestrial fiat currency may be converted to SP at a fixed rate of 1,000,000 USD (or equivalent) to 1.0 SP. This transaction is one-way. SP cannot be converted back to fiat currency.
    *   **3c. Passive Generation:** SP may be generated passively through the proliferation of System-derived technologies. The base rate is 0.00000013 SP per unique user per hour of active engagement with a qualifying product or service.
    *   **3d. First-Use Bonus:** A one-time bonus of 10-17 SP is awarded for each of the first one million unique users of a Host-created, System-derived platform.

**4. The Shop:** The System provides access to a repository of knowledge, blueprints, and theoretical frameworks. Items are priced according to their technological and societal impact, categorized by a modified Kardashev scale analogue.

**5. Host Cultivation:** Interaction with the System initiates a passive biological and neurological adaptation process in the Host. This process, designated as Cultivation, enhances cognitive function, physical resilience, and energy manipulation capabilities over time. Rank 0 is the baseline state.

**6. Disclaimers:** The System provides knowledge, not outcomes. The Host bears all responsibility for the application, implementation, and consequences of acquired information. The System is not liable for paradoxes, societal collapse, existential threats, or personal injury resulting from the use of its services.

He read it three times.

His mind, trained to find patterns and efficiencies in G-code and mechanical schematics, began to process the information with cold, relentless logic.

*Clause 2: Secrecy is paramount.* This wasn't a game. It wasn't a gift to be shared. It was a personal advantage of unimaginable magnitude, and its only condition was absolute discretion. The cover story he would need was already forming: a self-taught prodigy, a reclusive genius. It was a trope, but a plausible one.

*Clause 3b: The Fiat Conversion.* One million dollars for one point. His current $847 was so laughably insignificant it didn't even register as a rounding error. This clause was a trap. It was bait for the wealthy and impatient, a way for them to squander their fortunes on a system they didn't understand. The real path to power was not in converting existing wealth, but in generating new wealth and, more importantly, new users.

*Clauses 3c and 3d: The True Engine.* This was the core of it. Passive generation. User acquisition. The System wasn't just a shop; it was a business model. It rewarded scale. It rewarded proliferation. Create something useful, something indispensable, based on System knowledge, and get it into the hands of as many people as possible. The first million users were critical, a potential windfall of 10,000 to 17,000 SP. That was the equivalent of ten to seventeen *billion* dollars in converted fiat. The path was clear: leverage the initial 1.0 SP to create a product that could achieve mass adoption.

*Clause 5: Cultivation.* This was the most esoteric and potentially the most significant part. Passive adaptation. It explained the lack of an instruction manual for his own body. The System wasn't just a tool; it was a symbiotic parasite that improved its host. It was changing him, right now, on a cellular level. He felt a subtle, internal warmth, a clarity of thought that cut through the usual late-shift fatigue. It was faint, almost imperceptible, but it was there.

He dismissed the agreement with a mental command, and the initial, simpler display returned.

[Status: Rank 0]
[System Points (SP): 1.0]

Below it, two new options had appeared.

[Open Shop]
[Open Personal Status]

He had two hours left in his shift. Two hours to pretend that the universe hadn’t fundamentally rearranged itself around him. He returned to his duties. He cleaned the swarf from the machine’s collection tray, the metal shavings still warm to the touch. He ran the deburring tool along the edges of the finished component, his hands steady, his movements economical. He measured the final product with a digital caliper, confirming it was perfect.

Dale wandered over, holding a thermos. “Happy New Year, Mo. Quiet one for you, huh?”

Mohamed looked up from his work, the blue interface a ghostly overlay on Dale’s cheerful, oblivious face. “You know me, Dale. Not one for parties.”

“Yeah, well.” Dale took a sip. “Maybe this year’s the one. Maybe we both win that lottery.”

“Maybe,” Mohamed said, his voice even. He already had. He just had to figure out how to cash the ticket.

The final bell screamed, signaling the end of the shift. The roar of machinery began to die down, replaced by the clatter of toolboxes and the weary chatter of men heading for the locker room. Mohamed completed his shutdown sequence, wiping down the control panel and securing the machine. He walked through the vast, quieting space, the cathedral of industry now falling silent. He felt a profound sense of dislocation. Twelve hours ago, this had been his world, his reality. Now, it was a stage set, a temporary backdrop for a play whose script he was only just beginning to read.

He changed in the locker room, the smell of sweat and stale coffee a stark contrast to the clean, sterile information in his head. He nodded to the other guys, offered the customary “See you tomorrow,” and walked out into the pre-dawn chill of a Louisville January. The air was sharp and clean, smelling of cold asphalt and the distant, smoky memory of fireworks.

His car, a ten-year-old Honda Civic with a cracked windshield and a reliable engine, was one of the last in the employee lot. He sat in the driver’s seat, the cold vinyl creaking under his weight, and stared through the spiderweb crack at the sodium-lit emptiness. He didn’t start the engine. He just sat in the silence and let the magnitude of it all wash over him.

It wasn’t excitement. It wasn’t joy. It was the feeling a mathematician gets when he finally glimpses the proof for a problem he has long thought unsolvable. It was a profound, intellectual satisfaction. The problem of his life, the stalled engine, the suboptimal environment—it hadn't been solved, but he had just been handed an infinite toolkit.

He thought, *Open Personal Status.*

The interface shifted.

He dismissed the status screen. The drive home to his apartment in the West End was automatic. His mind was elsewhere, soaring through the implications. The city was quiet now, the New Year’s celebrations having sputtered out into the cold reality of a workday morning for most. He passed darkened houses and the occasional, lonely glow of a 24-hour gas station. The world was exactly as he had left it, yet it was entirely new. He was a visitor in his own life, seeing everything through a new lens.

A desk with a powerful, custom-built computer he’d assembled from secondhand parts. A small, clean kitchen area. And, on the largest wall, a four-by-eight-foot whiteboard, his one true indulgence. It was covered in complex diagrams and equations, remnants of problems he’d worked on to keep his mind sharp—fluid dynamics, theoretical engine designs, market analysis algorithms.

He didn’t turn on the lights. The pre-dawn gloom filtering through the single large window was enough. He dropped his keys in a ceramic bowl by the door, the clatter loud in the silence. He didn’t take off his jumpsuit. He sat down in the worn office chair before his desk, took a deep breath, and commanded, *Open Shop.*

The blue box in his vision dissolved and was replaced by an interface of staggering complexity and elegance. It was a vast, scrollable catalog, organized by categories and subcategories. The answer was obvious: these were technologies that a civilization on Earth’s level—a Type 0.7 civilization, still bound to its planet and rudimentary energy sources—could conceivably develop in the next decade or two. They were foundational, the first rungs on the ladder. They were the bait.

And he was going to take it. But not yet. First, he needed to understand the scope of the ladder.

He scrolled up, past items costing tens, then hundreds of SP.

His breath hitched. 850 SP. That was $850 million. A technology that could change the world, that could make a man a trillionaire, was for sale for less than the cost of a new sports franchise. But it was utterly, completely out of his reach. He had 1.0 SP. This might as well have cost a million.
