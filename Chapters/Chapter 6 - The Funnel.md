Process up to 100 documents at once." The announcement brought back 14 dormant accounts. Twelve of them scanned a batch within the first hour. Nine of them converted to paid by Sunday night.

The first-use bonuses arrived in clusters — 13.2, 14.8, 11.6, 15.4, 12.7 — the familiar fluctuating accrual that Mohamed tracked in the notifications panel with micro-saccades, never letting the numbers linger on his screen long enough for Danielle to see them.

Sunday evening brought the final tally: 148 users, 32 paying customers, and $63,744 in committed annual revenue. The business account held $44,414.26 after the week's revenue, Stripe fees, the Google Ads spend, the February rent paid to Mr. Park on the first of the month, and $45.17 at the office supply store for a power strip, a box of paper clips, and a Mr. Coffee machine that Danielle had declared non-negotiable.

The SP balance was 1,259.105655245 SSP. The Advanced Document Intelligence Blueprint had cost 400. The first-use bonuses from 38 new users had added 523.1. The passive income had added 0.002817, a number so small it was invisible but that was, like the radiator's heat, accumulating — degree by degree, hour by hour, user by user — toward something that would eventually matter.

Mohamed wrote the numbers on the whiteboard. He erased the old line — 100 USERS, 50 PAYING, $100K — and wrote:

148 USERS. 32 PAYING. $63,744.
NEXT: 200. 50. $100K.

The whiteboard was the same one they had bought at the office supply store on January 26th. The markers were the same blue markers Danielle had used to write the original charter. The numbers were different. The numbers were moving. The plan was running, and the plan was ahead of schedule on users and behind schedule on revenue, and the gap between the two was the price per customer, which was $1,992, which was the number that did not change because changing it would change the story the numbers told, and the story was the thing that mattered, and the story was this: two people in a room above a laundromat, building a company one invoice at a time.

---

Danielle had gone home at nine, leaving Mohamed alone in the office at 11:30 PM with the radiator and the laundromat's after-hours hum and the blue line at the bottom of his vision.

He tried to talk to it.

Not with questions. He had tried questions before — "What are you?" and "Why me?" and "Hello?" — and the questions had returned nothing, and he had learned that the System did not answer questions the way a person answers questions, with context and intent and the reciprocal obligation of a conversation. The System answered with data. It answered with the shop, with the balance, with the passive income that ticked forward in fractions too small to feel. It answered by being there, consistently, without variation, without mood, without the fluctuations that made human communication unreliable and meaningful. The System was not unreliable. The System was not meaningful. The System was a machine, and the machine recorded, and the recording was its only language.

So he did not ask questions. He talked.

"The business is growing," he said, aloud, to the empty office. His voice sounded strange in the room — flat, unpracticed, the voice of a man who spent most of his hours in silence. "We have 148 users. Thirty-two paying customers. A company in Nashville signed up yesterday. Twenty-five users. One contract."

The blue line held its rhythm — steady, metronomic, neither faster nor slower in response to his words. It was not listening. It was not not-listening. It was displaying, the way a clock displays the time, without regard for who reads it.

"I bought a blueprint. Four hundred SP. Advanced document intelligence. The business needed it. A customer was going to leave. I bought it, and the customer stayed." He paused. "Is that what you want? Me to build things? Me to sell things? Me to grow?"

The pulse did not change.

"Are you watching?"

Nothing. No flicker, no shift, no pause. The same steady cadence it had maintained since the first night, as if his voice were weather and the interface were stone.

He sat in the silence for a long time. The radiator ticked. The laundromat's dryer finished its cycle with a thump and a click. The office was warm now, almost comfortable, the cold of the morning a memory that the radiator had slowly, stubbornly erased. He thought about the knowledge in his head — the segmentation algorithm, the voting mechanism, the multi-page reconstruction logic — and he thought about where it came from, and the answer was a door he did not open, and the door was blue, and the door said nothing, and the nothing was the only honest answer the System had ever given him, and the answer was: it does not matter where it came from. What matters is what you build with it.

He closed his laptop, put on his jacket, and turned off the light. The blue line stayed, pulsing in the dark, recording his departure, recording the time, recording everything and explaining nothing. Mohamed walked out into the February cold and drove home to the Preston Street duplex, where he lay on the bed and slept for the first time in thirty-nine hours. The sleep was deep and dreamless and absolute. When he woke, the blue line was still there, the balance was still there, the business was still running, and the laundromat below the office was still washing the clothes of Louisville. The world was exactly the same as when he closed his eyes — except for six more users, one more paying customer, and $11,952 more in committed revenue. The world did not stop when Mohamed Vance slept. The funnel kept filling. The System kept recording.
