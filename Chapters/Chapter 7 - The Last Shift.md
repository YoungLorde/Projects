The second week of February began the way the first week had ended — with Mohamed arriving at the Fourth Street office before the laundromat opened, before the radiator turned on, before the city of Louisville had fully committed to the idea of being awake.

He was there at five-forty-five on Monday morning because the support inbox had sixty-three emails in it, and sixty-three was a number that required attention before it became seventy, and seventy was a number that required panic. The ticketing system Danielle had deployed on Friday was working — every email now became a tracked ticket with a status badge and an auto-reply — but the system tracked the problem; it did not solve it. The tickets accumulated. The auto-replies bought time. Time was the resource Mohamed had the least of, and the resource he was about to have more of, because February 9th was his last shift at Keen American Built, and after February 9th, the hours he spent loading billets and checking tolerances would become hours spent on VanceFlow, and the conversion was not one-to-one. The CNC mill consumed eight hours per shift. VanceFlow could use every one of those eight hours and ask for more.

He worked through the tickets until seven, when the laundromat opened and the vibration came up through the floorboards and settled into his folding table like a second heartbeat. He worked through them until eight, when Danielle arrived with two cups of gas station coffee and a look that said she had been thinking about something on the drive over and was ready to say it.

"We need a third person," she said.

She set the coffee on his table. The cups were styrofoam, the coffee was burnt, and the gesture was the closest thing to a morning ritual they had established in the six days since the office opened. Mohamed picked up his cup. The heat seeped through the styrofoam and into his fingers, and the fingers were stiff from the cold and from the typing and from the particular kind of fatigue that came from sleeping four hours and working twenty.

"I know."

"For support. Not coding. Not sales. Support. Someone who can answer the basic questions — how to upload, how to export, how to add a user — so that you and I can focus on the things that actually require us."

"The basic questions are sixty percent of the inbox."

"Exactly. Sixty percent of the inbox is someone asking how to change their password or why their CSV export has commas instead of tabs. Those are not questions that require the founder's judgment. Those are questions that require a person who has read the documentation and can type a response in under five minutes."

Mohamed took a sip of the coffee. It tasted like hot water that had been angry at coffee beans. "Who?"

"I have someone."

He looked at her. Danielle's "someones" were not random. She did not suggest people lightly. She had suggested the Fourth Street office, and the office was right. She had suggested the ticketing system before the batch upload, and the ticketing system was right. "Marcus," she said.

"Marcus Webb?"

"Marcus Webb. He's been using VanceFlow since the second week. He knows the product better than anyone except us. A new panel had appeared since Friday, one he had not seen before. It was labeled SUPPORT LOAD, and it showed a line graph of ticket volume over time, with a trend line that rose steadily from left to right.

"Forty-seven tickets on Friday. Fifty-one on Saturday. Sixty-three this morning. The trend is linear, not exponential, but linear at this rate means a hundred tickets a day by the end of the month. You cannot answer a hundred tickets a day and write code and run a business. Something breaks, and the thing that breaks is either the support or the code, and if the support breaks, the funnel breaks, and if the code breaks, the product breaks, and both of those break the company."

The math was simple. The math was always simple. The math said: hire Marcus. The complication was not the math. Friday, February 9th. The last shift.

Mohamed arrived at Keen at 9:45 PM, fifteen minutes before the shift started, because he wanted to walk the floor one last time. Not for sentimentality. For inventory. He wanted to remember the place — the sodium-vapor light, the coolant smell, the scream of the spindle, the feel of the concrete floor through his boots — because the place was the origin of the product, and the product was the origin of the company, and the company was the origin of everything that came next, and origins were worth remembering even when you were happy to leave them.

The floor was the same. Dale was running the lathes, same as always, same gray beard, same jokes about winning the lottery and firing the supervisor. Two new guys were on the mills — replacements, Mohamed assumed, for the machinists who had left in the last month. The turnover at Keen was constant. People came, people loaded billets, people left. The machine did not care. The machine ran the same program whether the operator was a ten-year veteran or a first-day hire, and the program was the only thing that mattered, and the program was the thing that Mohamed had been a part of and was now leaving, and the leaving felt like stepping off a conveyor belt that had been carrying him in a direction he did not want to go.

He loaded the last billet at 10:15. It was a standard job — 6061 aluminum, 4-inch stock, a bracket for a customer whose name he had never known and would never know. The program ran for forty-three minutes. He monitored the cut, checked the tolerances, logged the results. Plus or minus five ten-thousandths of an inch. Within tolerance. The same words he had written in the log a thousand times, the same words that meant the part was acceptable, the same words that meant the machine had done its job and the machinist had done his job and the product was ready to move to the next stage of its journey through the manufacturing process, from raw stock to finished part to shipment to customer to whatever application the customer had designed it for, an application that the machinist would never see and never need to see, because the machinist's job was not to understand the whole system. The machinist's job was to make the part. And Mohamed had made the part, and the part was within tolerance, and the part was the last part he would make at Keen American Built.

He ran two more jobs through the night. A steel housing for an aerospace subcontractor. A brass fitting for a plumbing manufacturer. Not real in the sense that they had not been real before — every number since January 1st had been real, every user had been a person, every dollar had been a transaction — but real in the sense that they were now large enough to have weight, and the weight was the weight of a company that was no longer an experiment but an entity, an entity with three employees and an office and a product and a revenue stream and a growth rate that was not slowing down.

The number on the whiteboard crossed six figures for the first time on Thursday, February 13th, when a manufacturing company in Evansville, Indiana, signed a $1,992 annual contract that pushed the total past the line. Mohamed erased the old number and wrote the new one and stood back and looked at it and did not celebrate, because celebration was for endings and this was not an ending, but he did allow himself a moment — three seconds, no more — to stand in the cold office above the laundromat and look at the number $101,592 written in blue marker on a whiteboard and feel the weight of it, the weight of a hundred thousand dollars that did not exist a month and a half ago and that existed now because a man had sat in his apartment at midnight on New Year's Eve and seen a blue interface and decided to build something.

The SP balance climbed in parallel, a shadow economy that mirrored the public one. 1,259 SSP became 1,892 SSP as 52 new users generated first-use bonuses that fluctuated in their familiar range — 13.4, 14.7, 12.1, 15.3, 13.8, 11.9, 14.5, 12.6 — each one a small addition to a ledger that no one could see, each one a fraction of the currency that would, eventually, purchase the knowledge that would change the world, but not today, not this week, not this month. Today the currency was accumulating. Today the company was growing. Today the founder was coding and the partner was analyzing and the support person was answering emails and the laundromat below was washing clothes and the radiator was clanking and the world was turning and the System was silent and the silence was the shape of everything that came next.

Mohamed tried to talk to it on Sunday night. Not with questions. Not with gratitude. With a statement.

"I crossed a hundred thousand dollars in committed revenue this week."

The blue line pulsed.

"I hired someone. Marcus Webb. He started Monday. He cleared the inbox in one day. The company has three people now."

The blue line pulsed.

"I'm building QuickBooks export. The users need it. The funnel needs it. I'll ship it tomorrow."

The blue line pulsed. Steady. Metronomic. The same cadence it had maintained since the first night, as if his voice were weather and the interface were stone, and the stone did not care about the weather, and the weather did not need the stone to care.

He closed the laptop. He went home. He slept. And on Monday morning, he shipped the QuickBooks export, and the users who had been asking for it for two weeks found it in their dashboard, and twelve of them converted to paid within the first hour, and the funnel tightened another degree, and the numbers climbed another step, and the company that had started in a kitchen above a laundromat — no, in a kitchen in a duplex on Preston Street, and then in a room above a laundromat — continued to grow, one invoice at a time, one user at a time, one dollar at a time, one SP at a time, in the cold and the warmth and the silence and the sound of a city that did not know what was being built above its laundromats and inside its apartments and behind its closed doors, and would not know for a very long time, and the not-knowing was the point, and the point was the secret, and the secret was the shape of everything that came next.
