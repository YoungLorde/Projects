Mohamed Vance woke up on January 5th at 5:47 PM, fourteen minutes before his alarm, because his body had decided it was done sleeping.

The apartment was dark. The blue line at the bottom of his vision was still there. He expanded the System interface and looked at the balance: 28.400000245 SSP. He looked at it for a long time, the way a man might look at a photograph of someone he had not expected to see again.

Two days ago he had been broke. Now he had enough SP to buy the Web Application Security Fundamentals blueprint and still have eighteen SSP left over. The math was simple. The implication was not.

He got up, made coffee, and opened his laptop. VanceFlow's dashboard showed two users. Patricia had uploaded three invoices. Danielle had uploaded one, but her account showed seven attempts — she had been testing the edges, breaking things on purpose, cataloging the failures. He could see her fingerprints in the logs: she had tried handwritten notes, a receipt with a crease down the middle, a purchase order with a rotated page, and a PDF that was actually a screenshot of a spreadsheet.

She had found seven real bugs. She had not told him about any of them in the email. She had just listed the symptoms. The bugs were the subtext.

Mohamed smiled. It was a small, tight smile, the kind he had not made in years. Danielle Jones was a problem solver. She had looked at his tool and seen not what it was, but what it could be, and she had already begun the work of making it better without asking permission.

He would have to watch her. He would also have to hire her.

The System interface had a notifications panel he had not noticed before. It showed not just first-use bonuses, but usage events: documents uploaded, pages processed, confidence scores submitted. Every time a user engaged with VanceFlow, the System logged it. The passive income was tiny — 0.00000013 SP per user per hour at Rank 0 — but it was continuous, and it added up across days.

By January 9th, the passive income for the week was 0.000218 SSP. It was a fraction of a penny by the secret exchange rate, but it was proof of a principle. The users were working while he slept. Their attention was a currency, and the System was paying him for it.

On the morning of January 10th, Mohamed opened the System Shop and looked at the two blueprints he had been thinking about.

The first was the Small-Scale SaaS Marketing & Growth Blueprint, priced at 250 SSP. It promised landing-page optimization, B2B content marketing, email drips, referral loops, paid acquisition math, and LTV/churn analysis. It was the blueprint that would let him turn VanceFlow from a tool used by a few people into a product that could acquire customers on its own.

The second was Web Application Security Fundamentals, priced at 100 SSP. It was less exciting. It was the kind of purchase that did not make the product better at what users saw. It made the product safer at what they did not see.

He had 28.4 SSP from the first two users plus the week of passive income, but he had only nine users. Buying the marketing blueprint would leave him with almost nothing and no ability to buy security if something went wrong. Buying the security blueprint would leave him with 18.4 SSP and a safer product, but no clear path to more customers.

He thought about the choice the way he thought about a machining setup. Marketing was the feed rate — it determined how fast the business grew. Security was the clamp — it held the workpiece in place while the cut happened. If the clamp was loose, the cut would fail. If the feed was too slow, the job would take forever. But the clamp had to come first. You could not speed up a cut that was throwing the part across the room.

He bought Web Application Security Fundamentals.

The blueprint cost 100 SSP. It was the most expensive thing he had ever bought, and it was still the cheapest security education he could have acquired. The knowledge poured into him: secure authentication, password hashing, input sanitization, HTTPS, database encryption, session management, basic intrusion detection. He understood, in the same way he had understood the document processing blueprint, not just the concepts but the implementation details. He knew which libraries to use. He knew which parameters to set. He knew the common mistakes that turned a secure system into a breached one.

He spent the rest of the day hardening VanceFlow. He added hashed passwords, replacing the plain-text passwords he had used in the prototype because he had not known better. He added parameterized queries, closing the SQL injection holes that Danielle had apparently noticed before he had. He forced HTTPS, redirecting every HTTP request to the secure version. He added rate limiting so that a single user could not brute-force a login. He added a session timeout so that forgotten tabs would not stay logged in forever. He wrote a short privacy policy and a terms of service that did not promise more than the software could deliver.

He also added logging. It was a detail that users would never see, but it would tell him if anyone was probing the system. He added alerts for failed login attempts, for unusual upload patterns, for access from unexpected IP addresses. He told himself this was normal security hygiene. A deeper part of him knew it was paranoia. The System was a secret that could not be explained. If anyone tried to find the source of VanceFlow's capabilities, the logs would be his first warning.

The work was tedious and necessary. Mohamed did it with the patience of a man who had spent his life doing tedious, necessary things. Every line of code was a wall. Every wall made the secret safer. If VanceFlow was ever investigated, if anyone ever tried to find the source of the technology, the security would not stop them. But it would slow them down, and time was the only defense he had.

He pushed the changes at 9:47 PM. Danielle emailed him at 11:34 PM: *You fixed the SQL injection problem. Finally.*

Mohamed read the email twice. He had not told her he was fixing security. She had inferred it from the commit history on the GitHub repo he had shared with her. She was watching him as closely as he was watching her.

He replied: *I told you I read a lot.*

Her response came two minutes later: *Nobody reads this fast. But I'll take it.*
