The CNC lathe screamed its last cut of the year, and Mohamed Vance let the sound wash through him like a lullaby he had learned to hate.

He stood five-foot-six in steel-toed boots that had worn thin at the heel, a dark-complexioned man in grease-stained coveralls, watching the machine spit a finished axle coupler into its catch tray. The shop floor of Keen American Built smelled of cutting fluid, scorched metal, and the stale coffee someone had left on the bridgeport mill. It was eleven-forty on New Year's Eve. The other two machinists had already clocked out, gone to bars on Bardstown Road or to friends' apartments where the beer would be warm and the countdown loud. Mohamed had stayed. The graveyard shift paid time-and-a-half for holiday hours, and his checking account held exactly one hundred forty-two dollars and nineteen cents.

The shop was a cavern of concrete and steel, lit by rows of fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency only the young could hear. The machines were arranged in aisles like pews in a church no one had built for beauty. Lathes, mills, grinders, a waterjet that had been out of order since March. The walls were painted institutional green up to shoulder height, then bare concrete to the ceiling. A calendar from a tooling supplier hung next to the time clock, still showing November. No one had bothered to flip it.

He couldn't afford to celebrate.

He pulled the part, ran his thumb over the threads, and set it in the inspection bin. The QC tag read *within tolerance*. Most things in Mohamed's life were within tolerance. His apartment was within tolerance. His job was within tolerance. His existence was a long string of acceptable deviations from a blueprint he had never been allowed to see.

The floor supervisor, a man named DeWitt with a mustache that had not changed in six years, appeared at the end of the aisle. He was wearing a jacket already, keys in hand. Behind him, the Haas mill and the bridgeport sat in their usual poses, old machines that had outlived every operator who had ever complained about them. Mohamed had a strange affection for them. They did not pretend. They did not promise. They either cut true or they did not, and the reason was always mechanical.

"Vance," he said, not unkindly. "You sure you want the hours? You been here since six."

"I'm sure, Mr. DeWitt."

DeWitt nodded, the way a man nods when he has already stopped listening. "Happy New Year, then. Try not to set anything on fire."

"I'll do my best."

DeWitt left. The door to the front office clicked shut, and then the only sounds were the machines cooling and the fluorescent lights. Mohamed was alone on the floor, a small figure surrounded by three million dollars of industrial equipment that had been manufactured before he was born.

He cleaned his station with the mechanical precision of a man who had done it two thousand times. Wipe the chuck. Sweep the chips. Power down the coolant pump. He checked the oil level on the Haas mill because DeWitt never did, and wrote the number on the log sheet with a pencil stub. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, and somewhere outside the loading dock a firework popped early, a thin red crack above the Louisville skyline.

He had learned, over five years at Keen, that the machines told the truth. A part was good or bad. A setup was square or it was not. There was no politics in machining, no favoritism, no hidden agenda. The metal did not care about his grief or his poverty or the degree he had almost earned. It only cared about the cut. That was why he stayed. The floor was honest in a way the rest of the world was not.

Mohamed didn't look up.

He had learned not to look up at fireworks. They were a reminder of years, and years were a reminder of time, and time was a reminder that he was twenty-six years old with a two-year associate's degree, no savings, and a résumé that ended at a machine shop on the south side of Louisville. He had been good at math once. He had been good at circuits and thermodynamics and the elegant puzzle of making things work. He had been two semesters away from an electrical engineering degree before the thing with his parents, before the funeral, before the academic suspension that became a withdrawal that became a life.

He didn't think about it often. Thinking about it did not pay rent.

But tonight, alone on the floor with the machines cooling around him, the old thoughts crept in. His father's voice, Kenyan and precise, reading him engineering textbooks at the kitchen table. His mother's laugh when he took apart the toaster and put it back together wrong. The way they had both looked at him the night before the accident, as if he were already someone important, someone who would build bridges or design power grids or maybe, if he was lucky, change the world in a small, decent way.

The accident had taken them three days later. A semi-truck had crossed the median on I-64. Mohamed had been in class. He had not even known until the dean called him out of a thermodynamics lecture.

That was seven years ago. The grief had settled into a low-grade fever, a constant background temperature he had learned to work through. He had not gone back to school. He had not made new friends. He had found a job at Keen American Built, where the work was hard enough to keep his hands busy and his mind too tired to wander. It had been a survival strategy. It had become a life.

He finished the log sheet and set the pencil in the drawer. The clock above the time clock read 11:52. Eight minutes until the new year, and he was the only person in the building who knew or cared.

The grief had been a country he moved into without a map. He had sold his parents' car to pay the funeral costs. He had withdrawn from school because he could not sit in a classroom and pretend that the laws of physics still mattered while the two people who had taught him to love them were in the ground. He had survived by becoming small. He had survived by not wanting anything. The wanting had been the danger — the wanting had been the thing that made the loss unbearable, because it pointed at the gap between what he had and what he had expected, and the gap was where the grief lived.

Tonight, walking home with coffee cooling in his hand, he felt the old want stirring. Not for his parents back — that was a want too large to hold — but for the person he had been before the accident. The boy who believed he could build a ladder to the moon. The boy who had not yet learned that most people spent their lives inside gray concrete boxes and never looked up.

Mohamed's apartment was the second floor of a duplex on Preston Street, above a landlord who watched television until three in the morning. The stairs creaked under his boots. The hallway smelled like the landlord's cigarettes and the neighbor's fried fish. He let himself in, locked the door with two clicks, and stood in the dark for a moment, listening to the furnace rattle.

One hundred thirty-seven dollars and thirty cents.

Rent was due in six days.
