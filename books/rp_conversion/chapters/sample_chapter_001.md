# Chapter 1: Day Zero

The smell hit first. That was the thing nobody talked about afterward, when survivors traded stories in hushed voices behind makeshift barricades and tried to piece together a shared narrative of the end of everything they had ever known. They talked about the sky cracking, about the light, about the System interface burning itself into their vision like a brand pressed against the inside of their skulls. But nobody mentioned the smell, because the smell was the part that made it real, the part that transformed the catastrophe from something cinematic and almost beautiful in its cosmic scale into something intimate and grotesque and impossibly human. It was ozone and burning copper and something older, something that Mohamed Vance's hindbrain identified as wrong before his conscious mind could parse what his nostrils were telling him. It was the smell of the atmosphere itself being peeled open, layer by layer, like the skin of a fruit that had never been meant to be eaten.

Mohamed was twenty-four years old, and he was standing on the corner of East 149th Street and Grand Concourse in the South Bronx, holding a plastic bag from the bodega three blocks east that contained exactly three cans of black beans, a loaf of white bread that was one day from its sell-by date and therefore forty cents cheaper, and a bottle of El Yucateco green hot sauce that he had debated purchasing for nearly two full minutes while the cashier — a Dominican woman named Rosa who had been selling him loosies and Arizona iced teas since he was fourteen — watched him with the patient, mildly contemptuous expression of a woman who had seen ten thousand young men stand in her store agonizing over purchases that cost less than a subway fare. The hot sauce won, as it always did, because Mohamed had learned early and painfully that the distance between a meal you could tolerate and a meal you could enjoy was often measured in condiments, and that four dollars and thirty-seven cents spent on flavor was not an indulgence but an investment in the kind of small, daily pleasure that kept a man from hollowing out entirely in a city that charged rent on the air you breathed.

Then the sky cracked, and four dollars and thirty-seven cents stopped meaning anything at all.

The fracture began directly overhead, which meant that the eight-point-two million residents of New York City had a front-row seat to what astrophysicists would later describe — in the frantic, half-coherent papers they scrawled in bunkers before the power grids failed — as a localized dimensional membrane rupture, though that clinical terminology would have meant nothing to Mohamed, who saw only light. Impossible, geometry-defying light that split the October sky the way a hammer splits a pane of glass, sending jagged white fissures racing from horizon to horizon in patterns that his brain insisted were random but his gut told him were deliberate, purposeful, designed by something that operated on a scale so far beyond human comprehension that the very act of witnessing it felt like an insult to the limitations of his biology. The fissures widened, and through them he could see stars — thousands of them, millions, a density of stellar light that no earthbound telescope had ever captured because it belonged to a region of space that did not exist on any chart, in any database, in any model of the universe that humanity had ever constructed.

The sound came next, though "sound" was an inadequate word for what washed over the South Bronx at 2:47 PM Eastern Standard Time on a Tuesday afternoon in October. It was not audible in the traditional sense; it did not travel through air and strike eardrums and vibrate cochlear membranes. It propagated through something more fundamental — through bone, through the fluid in his joints, through the electrical impulses running along his nervous system, through the quantum states of the atoms that constituted his body. Mohamed felt it in the roots of his teeth and in the spaces between his vertebrae and in the pit of his stomach, a subsonic vibration that resonated at a frequency that seemed to communicate a single, wordless message: everything you know is over, and what comes next does not care whether you are ready.

He dropped the groceries. The plastic bag hit the sidewalk and the hot sauce bottle shattered, sending a spray of green liquid across the concrete in a pattern that looked almost like the fissures spreading across the sky above — jagged, branching, reaching outward from a central point of impact. Mohamed would think about that later, in the dark hours between midnight and dawn when sleep refused to come and his mind cycled through the last moments of the old world with the obsessive, grinding repetition of a machine that could not be turned off. He would think about how the last normal thing he ever did was waste four dollars and thirty-seven cents on a sidewalk in the Bronx, and how the shattered bottle had looked like a prophecy written in hot sauce and glass, a miniature model of the fracture that was, at that very moment, rewriting the rules of existence for every living creature on Earth.

Around him, the street was experiencing its own apocalypse in microcosm. A woman in a charcoal business suit had frozen mid-stride, her right foot still lifted from the pavement, her briefcase dangling from fingers that had gone white-knuckled with a grip that was no longer conscious but purely autonomic, the body's ancient and inadequate response to a threat that no evolutionary pressure had ever prepared it to face. An old man with an aluminum walker stood in front of the check-cashing place on the corner, his mouth open, his dentures slightly askew, his rheumy eyes reflecting the geometric light show overhead with an expression that Mohamed recognized from his own grandmother's face in the months before she died — the look of someone who had lived long enough to stop being surprised by the world's capacity for upheaval but who was, in this final cosmic joke, surprised anyway. A teenager on a BMX bicycle had stopped in the middle of the intersection, one foot on the curb, the other on a pedal, balanced in an equilibrium that seemed to mirror the larger balance being disrupted above — the boy's face was slack, his earbuds dangling from one ear, the tinny ghost of a reggaeton beat still audible in the silence that had swallowed the city's perpetual noise. A UPS driver stood beside his brown truck, one hand on the open door, the other holding a package addressed to someone who would never sign for it, and he was crying — not sobbing, not wailing, just silently leaking tears from eyes that could not process what they were seeing and had chosen, in the absence of a rational response, to simply overflow.

The teenager had fallen off his bicycle and was curled on the asphalt in the fetal position, his body making itself as small as possible, as if reduction in surface area might somehow reduce the scope of what was being done to him. The UPS driver had slumped against his truck, his hand still gripping the undelivered package, his tears replaced by a blank, thousand-yard stare that Mohamed had seen before in documentary footage of soldiers and disaster survivors — the face of a nervous system that had exceeded its capacity for input and had simply stopped trying to process.
